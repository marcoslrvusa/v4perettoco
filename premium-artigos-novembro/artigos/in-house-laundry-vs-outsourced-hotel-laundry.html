In-House Laundry vs Outsourced Hotel Laundry: Cost, Linen Life and Turnaround

A cost model, contract checklist and par-level plan for deciding where hotel linen gets washed

A47.316 palavras30 minQuery: hotel laundry outsourcingDestino: /hotel-housekeeping-services

Direct answer: Hotel laundry outsourcing usually costs $0.85 to $1.60 per pound of linen in the New Jersey and New York market, against a fully loaded in-house cost of $0.70 to $1.30 per pound for properties above roughly 120 rooms. Below 80 rooms, in-house almost always exceeds $1.50 per pound once labor, utilities, chemicals, space and linen replacement are counted.

Key takeaways

  • In-house laundry is only cheaper at scale: a 150-room property that runs 300 occupied rooms a day can wash at $0.90 per pound, while a 70-room property washing the same linen in a conventional washer-extractor typically lands above $1.60.
  • The visible cost of an in-house laundry is labor and utilities. The cost that decides the question is linen life, and it moves more than any negotiated rate.
  • Linen replacement is not a laundry expense on most P&Ls, which is why in-house operations look cheaper than they are by 20% to 35%.
  • Par level, not price per pound, is what protects room availability. The formula is turns per day times lead time in days plus safety stock, and most hotels run 0.8 to 1.5 par short of it.
  • Outsourcing transfers capital, floor space and continuity risk, but only if the contract names weights, turnaround windows, rejection criteria and linen accountability.

1. What does hotel laundry actually cost per pound, in-house versus outsourced?

Every property that has debated outsourcing has run the same first calculation: the price per pound from a commercial laundry against the cost of the chemicals and wages the hotel already pays. That comparison is wrong in one direction and incomplete in the other, and it produces a decision that looks financially obvious for about eighteen months.

The right unit is a fully loaded cost per pound of processed linen, on both sides of the comparison. On the in-house side, fully loaded means labor including burden, utilities, water and sewer, chemicals, equipment depreciation, maintenance and parts, linen replacement attributable to wash damage, floor space, and the management time consumed by running a small industrial operation inside a hospitality business. On the outsourced side, it means the invoice plus freight, minimums, surcharges, replacement charges and the labor your own team still spends handling, sorting and moving linen.

Typical operating ranges in the US market sit where the answer box says. A property above 120 rooms with a tunnel washer, an efficient flow and a stable crew usually lands between $0.70 and $1.00 per pound fully loaded. A property between 80 and 120 rooms running a conventional washer-extractor lands between $1.00 and $1.30. A property below 80 rooms, or one washing food and beverage linen alongside rooms linen, routinely exceeds $1.50 per pound, and that is before linen replacement is honestly allocated.

Commercial laundry pricing in the New Jersey and New York metro typically runs $0.85 to $1.60 per pound for rooms linen, with terry and food and beverage linen at the upper end because of weight, soil and finishing requirements. The overlap between the two ranges is where the real analysis lives: a 110-room property can plausibly go either direction, and the decision then turns on linen life, space and risk rather than on the rate itself.

Two structural points frame everything that follows.

The comparison must use the same linen mix. A hotel that washes sheets and towels in-house and sends table linen out has a blended cost that cannot be compared to an all-in outsourcing quote. Normalize to pounds of rooms linen before comparing, or the cheaper option will always appear to be the one with the narrower scope.

The comparison must use the same accounting period. Equipment replacement, boiler work and linen replacement are lumpy. A single month of in-house cost makes a twenty-year-old washer-extractor look efficient right up to the quarter it fails. Use a rolling twelve months, and include a reserve for capital.

Once both sides are normalized, most properties discover the gap is smaller than the first spreadsheet suggested, and the conversation becomes about what each model actually protects.

2. Why does an in-house laundry look cheaper on the P&L than it is?

Hotel accounting tends to scatter the true cost of an in-house laundry across five or six lines, none of which carries the word laundry. That is not fraud; it is how departmental reporting evolved. It does, however, make the in-house option systematically look better than it is in any comparison built from the general ledger.

The largest distortion is linen replacement. On many properties, replacement is charged to rooms expense or to operating supplies, not to laundry, and it is approved in batches when towels start failing inspection. Because the laundry is what destroys linen, the cost belongs there. A property that replaces 3% of its towel inventory every year at $45,000 in annual replacement is carrying roughly $3.75 per occupied room-night of laundry-driven cost that never appears in the laundry line.

The second distortion is labor that is not coded to laundry. A housekeeping utility person who moves linen carts, a houseman who loads the washers between floor assignments, a supervisor who spends two hours a day chasing shortages: none of that appears in a laundry labor account, and all of it disappears the moment the work is outsourced. Estimates of uncoded labor in small in-house laundries commonly run 15% to 25% of the coded laundry payroll.

The third distortion is space. A laundry that occupies 1,200 square feet of a hotel in a market where function space or back-of-house storage is scarce has an opportunity cost that no P&L line captures. In urban New Jersey and New York properties, that square footage is frequently worth more per year than the entire laundry labor saving it is supposedly protecting.

The fourth distortion is utilities allocated by square footage. A laundry in a 1,200-square-foot room might receive 4% of the building's utility bill when it actually consumes 12% to 18% of the gas and hot water, especially in properties still running conventional washer-extractors with gas dryers.

The fifth distortion is maintenance and capital. Washer-extractors, dryers, ironers and boilers fail predictably. A property that has not replaced an ironer in fifteen years has been consuming capital silently, and the reserve required to fund the next replacement is a real annual cost that almost never appears in the operating comparison.

The sixth distortion is the one that shows up on guest surveys instead of the P&L: quality variance during peak. When the laundry is internal and the crew is short on a Saturday with 100 departures, the hotel does not buy more laundry capacity. It sends out what it can, holds back what it cannot, and puts slightly damp or slightly grey linen on the floor. That cost is real and invisible.

Add these six together and the true in-house cost typically runs 20% to 35% above what the property believes it is spending. 3. Which hidden costs sit inside an in-house laundry operation?

Section 2 argued that the in-house number is understated. This section puts structure on it, because a facilities director who can name the cost components can defend a decision to ownership, and one who cannot will lose the argument to whoever presents the simpler spreadsheet.

Two observations about that table matter more than the percentages.

The three largest controllable levers are labor productivity, wash chemistry and dryer discipline. Labor productivity in a small laundry is capped by equipment: a single 60-pound washer-extractor processing 12 loads a shift cannot be made dramatically more productive by working faster. Wash chemistry and drying, by contrast, are where linen life is decided, and linen life is 8% to 15% of cost that most properties never manage deliberately.

The largest uncontrollable lever is scale. Pounds per labor hour in a well-run in-house laundry typically run 55 to 75 above 120 rooms, 35 to 55 between 80 and 120 rooms, and 20 to 35 below 80 rooms. That curve, not managerial skill, is why small properties struggle to justify in-house operations. It is also why a small property that outsources is not admitting failure; it is acknowledging a fixed-cost structure it cannot amortize.

There is a third observation that rarely appears in cost models. An in-house laundry consumes management attention disproportionate to its size. It has its own scheduling, its own call-outs, its own equipment emergencies, its own chemical supplier relationships and its own quality disputes with the floors. For a general manager, that attention has a price, and in a 90-room property it is frequently the most expensive line in the analysis.

4. What should a laundry outsourcing contract specify?

A laundry contract is a manufacturing agreement disguised as a service agreement. The vendor is promising to convert soiled linen into clean linen at a defined quality, at a defined rate, within a defined window. Every clause that does not name a number, a test or a remedy will be resolved in the vendor's favor, not because vendors are dishonest but because ambiguity is resolved by whoever holds the operational data.

Seven clauses carry most of the risk.

Weight basis and how it is measured. Contracts must state whether billing is on soiled weight in, clean weight out, or a formula that reconciles the difference. Weight loss of 2% to 5% is normal through washing and drying; a contract that bills on soiled weight without a reconciliation clause invites permanent overbilling, and one that bills on clean weight without a moisture standard invites under-delivery.

Minimums and how they bind. Most commercial laundries set a weekly minimum in pounds or dollars. A property with seasonal occupancy needs to know whether the minimum applies in February, and whether unused minimums can be banked into peak weeks.

Turnaround window, stated in hours and days of week. "Twenty-four to forty-eight hours" is not a service level. The contract should say: soiled picked up before 7 am, clean delivered by 6 am the following day, seven days a week, with a separate stated window for holiday weeks.

Rejection criteria with objective tests. Stain acceptance, acceptable grey level, moisture content, fold and presentation standard, and what happens to rejected pieces. Without a written reject process, rejected linen simply disappears into the vendor's cycle and returns dirty, late, or not at all.

Linen accountability and replacement liability. Who owns the inventory, who counts it, how often, and what the vendor pays when pieces are lost or destroyed. A common structure holds the vendor liable for losses above an agreed annual variance, with the count performed monthly on a stated sample or full inventory.

Rate structure and escalation. Price per pound by category, plus fuel surcharge, plus delivery, plus any per-piece finishing charge. Escalation should be tied to a published index with a cap, not to a conversation.

Exit and transition. Notice period, obligation to return inventory, obligation to continue service during transition, and access to weight and count records. Properties that skip this clause discover in month eleven that the only leverage they have is a contract that is about to expire.

5. How is outsourced hotel laundry priced, and what moves the price?

Published rates are almost always a starting point rather than the number a property pays. Understanding what moves a quote is what allows a facilities director to compare two proposals that look identical on the first line.

Category mix. Sheets, duvet covers, pillowcases, bath towels, hand towels, washcloths, bath mats, pool towels and food and beverage linen price differently. Food and beverage napkins and tablecloths carry higher rates because of soil level and finishing. A quote built on a rooms-only mix will not hold when the hotel adds a banquet operation.

Soil level and property tier. A select-service property with a conservative linen reuse program generates lighter soil than a luxury property with daily linen change, turndown and spa towels. Vendors price soil because soil drives chemistry, water and rework.

Distance and route density. A property on a dense route with several accounts is cheaper to serve than one forty minutes from the plant. Route density, not distance alone, determines the drop fee. A property that shares a route with three other hotels in the same county has negotiating room that an isolated property does not.

Delivery frequency. Seven-day service costs more than five-day service, and twice-daily service costs materially more. A property that can hold 1.2 par can often negotiate five-day delivery and absorb the difference in inventory rather than in rate.

Contract length and volume commitment. Multi-year commitments with volume floors buy rate. The trade is flexibility, and the analysis should price that flexibility explicitly: what does the property pay per pound for the option to walk away in twelve months?

Finishing requirements. Flatwork ironing for sheets and tablecloths is a separate cost from washing and drying. A property that accepts folded sheets instead of ironed sheets saves money and changes its floor presentation; that is a brand and guest-experience decision that should be made deliberately.

Reconciliation and reporting. Vendors that provide per-category weight reporting, photo-documented rejects and monthly inventory reconciliation price slightly higher than vendors that provide a monthly invoice. The reporting is worth the premium because it is the only way the property can audit what it is buying.

A useful discipline: ask every vendor for the same five-line rate card, covering sheets, duvet covers, terry, food and beverage linen and finishing, plus the surcharge schedule. Proposals that cannot be expressed in that format are proposals the property cannot compare, and a hospitality cleaning partner in New Jersey /hospitality-cleaning-services-nj that prices housekeeping and laundry together should be able to show the laundry line on the same basis.

6. How does laundry handling affect linen life and replacement cost?

Linen life is the single most under-managed number in hotel laundry, and it is where the in-house versus outsourced decision is actually won or lost. Two properties paying the same rate per pound can differ by 40% in annual replacement cost, and the difference is almost never the soap.

Commercial wash processes degrade textiles through four mechanisms: mechanical action, thermal shock, chemical damage and over-drying. Each is manageable, and each is managed differently in a hotel laundry than in a commercial plant.

Mechanical action. Conventional washer-extractors subject linen to repeated high-speed reversals. Tunnel washers move linen through discreet modules with gentler handling and lower water use. The difference in fabric stress is real, and it shows up as longer sheet life in tunnel-washed linen at equal chemistry.

Thermal shock. Washing at 160°F and then rinsing cold is efficient for soil removal and hard on fibers. Consistent temperature control, rather than maximum temperature, is what extends life. Many hotel laundries run hotter than necessary because hot water masks chemistry problems.

Chemical damage. Residual bleach, incorrect pH in the rinse, and overdosed alkali all shorten life. A wash chemistry program without titration testing and dosing verification is a linen destruction program with a monthly invoice. This is the most common cause of premature greying and fiber failure in hotel linen, and it is invisible until towels start failing at the edges.

Over-drying. Terry that reaches 5% moisture rather than 12% loses tensile strength faster and feels harsh. Dryer timers set by habit rather than by moisture measurement are one of the cheapest problems to fix and one of the most commonly ignored.

The arithmetic in the last column is the argument. A king sheet set at $50 replaced after 100 washes costs $0.50 per use; the same sheet lasting 150 washes costs $0.33. On a 150-room property running 80% occupancy, that difference is roughly 43,800 sheet-uses a year, or about $7,400 in annual replacement cost — from a change in wash process, with no change in the purchase price of linen.

Three practical rules follow.

Track washes, not months. Linen life is measured in cycles. A property that replaces on a calendar schedule replaces the wrong linen at the wrong time and keeps the worn pieces in circulation.

Inspect on a cycle, not on a complaint. A monthly inspection of 30 random pieces per category, scored for grey level, edge wear and holes, produces a replacement forecast that can be budgeted. Without it, replacement is an emergency purchase at retail pricing.

Separate damage from wear. Wear is expected; damage is a process failure. Holes along a fold line, brown spots from rust, and sudden greying across a batch all indicate a process problem that will repeat until the chemistry or equipment is corrected. 7. What par levels do you need to run a hotel without laundry interruptions?

Par is the inventory buffer that makes laundry invisible to the guest. It is also the number most often set by memory and adjusted by crisis. The formula is simple enough to run on a spreadsheet and specific enough to settle arguments.

Par per item = (turns per day × total cycle days) + safety stock

Total cycle days means the time from when a piece leaves the room to when it is available again: pickup, transport, wash, finish, return, and staging back to the floor. In-house same-day laundry typically runs a one-day cycle. Outsourced laundry with next-day delivery runs a two-day cycle, and a three-day cycle is common when delivery skips a day.

A 150-room property at 80% occupancy consumes roughly 120 room-sets per day. At a 3.0 multiplier, that property needs 360 sheet sets in circulation: 120 on beds, 120 in process, 120 in the linen room as buffer. A property running 2.4 par is running 288 sets, which means any disruption — a missed pickup, a washer down, a holiday — converts directly into a room not being available.

Three warnings about par.

Par is not a place to save money. Linen inventory is working capital, but the cost of being one par short is a room out of service, which on a $189 ADR night is more expensive than the inventory. Properties that cut par to improve a balance sheet usually restore it within two quarters.

Par must be calculated per item, not per room. Sheet sets, duvet covers and pillowcases turn at different rates, and towels turn faster than sheets because guests use more than one. A single "three par" target applied across categories guarantees some items run short and others accumulate.

Par must be stress-tested against the worst week, not the average week. Run the calculation against the property's highest three-day stretch of the year plus a holiday weekend. If the answer requires more par than the linen room can hold, that is a scheduling and storage decision, not a linen decision.

Par interacts directly with the outsourcing decision: a five-day delivery contract requires roughly one additional par compared with daily delivery, and that additional par has a cost. The cheapest rate per pound is frequently not the cheapest contract once par is priced in, which is the arithmetic most proposals quietly omit.

8. How long does laundry turnaround take, and where does it break?

Turnaround is the interval between a soiled piece leaving the floor and a clean piece being available for the next room. Every property states a number; far fewer measure it. The gap between the stated and the actual number is where room availability is lost.

In-house same-day laundry, when it runs well, achieves a 6 to 12 hour turnaround: floors stripped by 10 am, washed and dried by 4 pm, folded and staged by 6 pm. That speed is the main operational argument for keeping laundry in-house, and it is a real advantage in properties with tight par and high occupancy.

Outsourced laundry with daily pickup and next-day delivery achieves 20 to 30 hours door to door. That is entirely workable with adequate par. The failure mode is not the average; it is the variance. A vendor that delivers at 6 am on Tuesday and 2 pm on Thursday has an average turnaround of 26 hours and an operational turnaround that breaks the floor on Thursdays.

Four things break turnaround, in order of frequency.

Pickup timing versus shift timing. A pickup scheduled at 11 am misses the linen stripped between 9 and 10 am by one hour and adds a full day to the cycle for that volume. Pickups should be scheduled against the housekeeping shift, not against the vendor's route convenience.

Weight thresholds. Many contracts allow the vendor to defer a pickup that does not meet a minimum weight, or to consolidate two pickups into one. Each consolidation adds a day to the cycle. The contract should state that a scheduled pickup happens regardless of weight.

Finishing bottlenecks at the plant. Flatwork ironing is a serial process. When a plant is at capacity, ironed items are delayed while terry continues. Properties that require ironed sheets should confirm the plant's flatwork capacity, not only its wash capacity.

Receiving at the hotel. Clean linen that arrives at 6 am and sits in the dock until 9 am because no one is scheduled to receive it has a 23-hour effective turnaround. Receiving is part of the laundry process, and it needs a named owner and a time.

The realistic numbers, which should drive the par calculation rather than the stated ones: in-house same-day with adequate equipment holds its 8 to 12 hours; in-house with constrained equipment drifts to 14 to 20 hours; outsourced daily service on a dense route runs 20 to 28 hours; outsourced five-day service runs 40 to 60 hours across the weekend; and a holiday week pushes outsourced turnaround to 60 to 84 hours. The par implication moves in step, from 2.5 to 3.0 in the best case up to 4.5 to 5.5 in the worst.

The practical rule: measure actual turnaround for four consecutive weeks before signing or renewing, using timestamped pickup and delivery records rather than the vendor's summary. A vendor that reports a 24-hour average but cannot produce timestamps is reporting an intention.

9. Which linens should you outsource and which should stay in-house?

The decision is rarely binary. Many properties run a hybrid, and the split is usually the most economically rational answer once each category is examined on its own terms.

Rooms linen: the strongest outsourcing candidate. High volume, standardized, bulky, and heavy to transport relative to value. Sheets, duvet covers, pillowcases and terry are exactly what commercial plants are built to process, and the volume is predictable enough to price.

Food and beverage linen: mixed. Napkins and tablecloths benefit from professional flatwork, and a hotel without an ironer will get better results outsourced. But banquet operations generate unpredictable spikes and heavy soil, and a property with a large banquet business may find the surge pricing worse than the in-house cost.

Spa and pool towels: usually outsourced, with separate treatment. These carry higher soil and chemical loads and often require separate handling to avoid cross-contamination with rooms linen. A contract that folds them into the general per-pound rate either overcharges the property or under-services the category.

Chef coats and uniforms: frequently outsourced separately. Uniform programs with per-piece pricing and employee accountability are a distinct service with different economics, and bundling them into a rooms linen contract usually muddies both.

Mops, mats and cleaning cloths: keep in-house or use a dedicated service. Microfiber programs require color-coded separation and specific wash chemistry. A general linen plant will not protect the separation, and cross-contamination between restroom and food-contact cloths is a health inspection risk.

Delicate or brand-specific items: keep in-house. Decorative pillows, throws, high-thread-count duvet covers with brand-specific finishing, and any item with a short replacement lead time are poor candidates. The savings per pound do not justify the replacement risk.

A useful hybrid for a 120 to 180 room property: outsource rooms linen and food and beverage flatwork, keep microfiber and mops in-house with a dedicated small machine, and run uniforms on a separate per-piece program. That configuration captures the scale economics where they exist and avoids the categories where outsourcing creates new problems.

10. What are the continuity risks of outsourcing hotel laundry?

Outsourcing converts a fixed cost into a variable cost and converts an operational risk into a contractual one. Both halves of that sentence are true, and the second half is routinely underweighted.

Single-plant dependency. Most commercial laundries in a metro market operate one or two plants. A boiler failure, a fire, a labor action or a water main break at that plant stops linen for every account on the route. In-house laundry has the same exposure in principle, but a hotel with its own equipment usually has a second machine, a parts relationship and the ability to run a reduced service. A hotel dependent on a single plant has none of that.

Route disruption. Snow, flooding, bridge closures and highway incidents affect routes differently by geography. A property in a flood-prone corridor should ask specifically what the vendor's contingency plan is and how it was tested.

Capacity during peak. Commercial plants sell capacity in advance. A property that adds 40 rooms of occupancy in July, or takes a large group in a month the plant has already committed, may find that its contract guarantees a rate but not a volume.

Labor action. Commercial laundry work is labor-intensive and, in parts of the New York metro, unionized. A work stoppage at a plant is a stoppage at every property on its route.

Contractor failure. Commercial laundries consolidate, refinance and close. A property that has sold its washers, converted the space and reduced housekeeping headcount has no fallback if the vendor fails.

Data and inventory opacity. When the laundry is in-house, the property can count its linen at any time. When it is outsourced, the property depends on the vendor's count, and inventory shrinkage of 3% to 8% annually is common and hard to attribute.

Four contractual and operational responses reduce these risks materially.

  1. Retain a minimum in-house capability. Many properties keep one washer-extractor and one dryer, not to run daily operations but to survive a two-week disruption. The cost of that retained capacity is small relative to the risk it absorbs.
  2. Require a documented contingency plan with a named alternate plant. Ask when it was last activated and what the resulting turnaround was.
  3. Negotiate a capacity guarantee in pounds per week, not just a rate. A rate without capacity is a price list, not a service commitment.
  4. Keep the linen inventory owned by the hotel. Vendor-owned inventory programs are convenient and expensive: the property loses visibility and pays a rental premium that compounds over the contract term.

11. How do you compare an in-house and outsourced proposal line by line?

The comparison fails when it is built as two totals. It works when it is built as two annual cash flows with the same categories, the same volumes and the same capital treatment. Below is the structure that survives a CFO review.

Step 1 — fix the volume. Pounds of rooms linen per year, computed from occupied rooms times pounds per occupied room. Typical ranges run 11 to 14 pounds per occupied room-night in select service, 13 to 18 in full service and upscale, and 16 to 22 in luxury with turndown and spa. Use twelve months of actual occupancy, not a budget.

Step 2 — price the in-house side fully loaded. Labor with burden, utilities by meter or submeter if available, chemicals, maintenance, capital reserve, space at market rent, linen replacement at the observed rate, and supervision allocation.

Step 3 — price the outsourced side fully. Invoice at contract rate plus surcharges, freight, minimum shortfalls in low season, linen replacement at the observed rate, retained in-house labor for sorting and receiving, retained space for staging, and contract management time.

Step 4 — treat capital explicitly. In-house requires a capital reserve for equipment replacement, typically $150,000 to $450,000 every twelve to eighteen years for a mid-size property. Outsourcing releases that capital but may require an inventory investment of one to two additional par. Both should appear in year one.

Step 5 — price the risk. Assign a value to the retained in-house capability each option requires. A property that keeps a backup washer under an outsourcing model is paying for insurance; a property that does not is accepting an unhedged risk. Either is defensible, but the number should be visible.

12. How does laundry performance get measured once it is running?

Whichever model a property chooses, the measurement regime is identical. A laundry that is not measured degrades quietly for two quarters and then produces a guest complaint spike that takes another quarter to explain. Five metrics, reviewed monthly, prevent that cycle.

KPIDefinitionHealthy rangeWhat a bad reading means

Replacement ratePieces retired ÷ inventory per year2% – 5% by categoryWash damage or inventory age

Two interpretive notes.

Reject rate and replacement rate must be read together. A low reject rate with a high replacement rate means the property is accepting linen that fails early. A high reject rate with a low replacement rate means the property is being disciplined about quality and the vendor is absorbing rework, which is the correct behavior to reward.

Cost per occupied room is the only metric that survives a volume change. Per-pound rates look stable while cost per occupied room climbs, because occupancy mix and linen consumption per guest shift over time. Reviewing cost per occupied room monthly catches those shifts before they compound.

The monthly review should take twenty minutes and should be built around exceptions: the three weeks with the worst turnaround, the category with the highest loss rate, and the two largest reject batches with photos attached. Averages in a laundry report conceal exactly the information a facilities director needs.

13. What changes operationally when a hotel moves from in-house to outsourced laundry?

The transition is a logistics project disguised as a procurement decision. Properties that treat it as procurement discover in week three that housekeeping is short of carts, the dock has no lockable staging area, and nobody scheduled a receiver for the 6 am delivery.

Six changes need to be planned before the first pickup.

Inventory. The property needs one to two additional par, a full physical count before transfer, and a documented baseline so that later loss claims are possible. Without a baseline count, the vendor's count becomes the truth by default.

Space. The laundry room converts to staging, sorting and storage. That is a smaller footprint but a different layout, with lockable clean linen storage, separate soiled holding and a route that does not cross clean and soiled streams — a health inspection point, not only an efficiency one.

Labor. Housekeeping loses the laundry crew and gains sorting, receiving and floor distribution work. Net headcount usually falls, but the residual roles are different and must be scheduled against pickup and delivery times rather than against the wash cycle.

Scheduling. Housekeeping start times often need to move. If clean linen arrives at 6 am, stripping needs to finish before the 7 am pickup, which means the floor shift starts earlier or the previous evening shift strips more.

Quality control. The property needs an acceptance process at the dock: a rapid check of count, moisture and visible stains, with a photo and a signature. Rejects accepted at the dock cannot be claimed later.

Contingency. The first two weeks should run with retained in-house capacity available, even if it is not used. That window is when process problems surface, and having a fallback converts a crisis into an inconvenience.

A common timeline: four weeks for contract and baseline count, two weeks for space and layout, one week of parallel running with both systems available, then cutover. Properties that compress this into ten days usually spend the next month recovering with rented linen and expedited deliveries, which costs more than the transition would have.

14. Is outsourcing hotel laundry worth it for a 90-room property?

For most properties in that size range, yes — with two conditions. The economics are driven less by the per-pound rate than by the fixed-cost structure that a small laundry cannot amortize.

A 90-room property at 75% occupancy runs roughly 67 occupied rooms a day, or about 24,500 a year. At 13 pounds per occupied room, that is approximately 319,000 pounds of rooms linen annually. Against that volume, an in-house operation carries a labor crew, a boiler, washers, dryers, an ironer, a chemical program and 1,000 square feet of space, all sized for peak rather than average. The pounds per labor hour curve makes the in-house cost per pound structurally higher at this volume, typically $1.35 to $1.75 fully loaded against an outsourced rate of $0.95 to $1.25 in a dense New Jersey route.

The two conditions are these.

Condition one: enough par and enough storage. Outsourcing at 90 rooms requires three to four par and lockable clean storage. A property with a small linen room and no dock may find that the inventory requirement consumes the savings in space.

Condition two: a credible vendor with route density. A single-property contract with a distant plant is a continuity risk and a rate risk. A property that can attach to a dense route, ideally through a cleaning partner that already serves several properties in the county, gets both better pricing and better recovery when something goes wrong.

Above roughly 180 to 200 rooms, the calculation frequently reverses, especially for properties with consistent year-round occupancy, available back-of-house space and a stable laundry crew. In that range, in-house can genuinely be cheaper per pound, and the decision turns on management attention and capital rather than on cost.

The honest summary: laundry is a manufacturing business. Hotels that run it well at scale should keep it. Hotels that run it as a peripheral operation are usually paying a premium they have never measured, and the hotel housekeeping services /hotel-housekeeping-services model that includes linen flow, par management and inspection is designed for exactly that situation.

FAQ

How much does hotel laundry outsourcing cost per pound?

In the New Jersey and New York market, rooms linen typically runs $0.85 to $1.60 per pound, with terry and food and beverage linen at the upper end. Dense routes and multi-year commitments sit at the lower end. Deliveries, fuel surcharges and minimums are usually separate lines.

At what hotel size does in-house laundry become cheaper?

Commonly above 120 rooms with a tunnel washer and stable occupancy, and clearly above 180 to 200 rooms. Below 80 rooms, in-house almost always exceeds $1.50 per pound fully loaded once labor, utilities, space and linen replacement are counted.

How many pounds of linen does a hotel room generate per night?

Typical ranges are 11 to 14 pounds per occupied room-night in select service, 13 to 18 in full service and upscale, and 16 to 22 in luxury properties with turndown service and spa operations.

How long does outsourced hotel laundry take to turn around?

Twenty to thirty hours door to door is normal for daily pickup and next-day delivery. Five-day service runs forty to sixty hours over a weekend, which is why it requires one additional par compared with daily delivery.

What is par level in hotel laundry?

Par is the total inventory of a linen item needed to keep rooms available. The formula is turns per day times total cycle days, plus safety stock. Most properties need 3.0 to 3.5 par for daily outsourced service and 2.5 to 3.0 for same-day in-house laundry.

Does outsourcing shorten linen life?

Not necessarily. Commercial plants using tunnel washers and controlled chemistry frequently extend linen life by 20% to 30% compared with a small in-house operation running conventional washer-extractors with unverified dosing. Linen life depends on wash process, not on who owns the machine.

Who owns the linen in an outsourcing contract?

The hotel should own it. Vendor-owned inventory programs reduce the property's capital outlay but eliminate visibility and typically carry a rental premium that compounds over the term. Ownership also makes loss claims possible.

What happens if the laundry vendor misses a delivery?

It depends entirely on the contract. A workable clause states the delivery window, a remedy for late delivery, and a credit structure. Without that clause, the property absorbs the cost in rooms taken out of service, which is far larger than any plausible credit.

Can a hotel split laundry between in-house and outsourced?

Yes, and many do. Rooms linen and food and beverage flatwork go out; microfiber, mops and delicate brand items stay in-house on a small dedicated machine. The hybrid captures scale economics where they exist and avoids the categories where outsourcing creates new risk.

How often should laundry costs be reviewed?

Monthly on cost per occupied room, quarterly on rate and surcharge reconciliation, and annually on the full in-house versus outsourced comparison. Reviewing only the invoice misses surcharge creep and linen loss, which are usually larger than rate changes.
