Quarry Tile Floor Care in Commercial Kitchens: Degreasing Without Damage

How to strip grease from quarry tile and grout without eating the floor, and the schedule that keeps it from coming back

D66.845 palavras29 minQuery: quarry tile kitchen floor cleaningDestino: /restaurant-commercial-cleaning

Direct answer: Quarry tile kitchen floor cleaning works when grease is removed in layers with an alkaline degreaser matched to the soil, a controlled dwell time, mechanical agitation and full water recovery. Acid, heavy caustic and high pressure clean the tile face while destroying the grout, and damaged grout is what makes the floor absorb grease faster.

Key takeaways

  • Quarry tile is unglazed extruded clay with roughly 1% to 5% water absorption; the tile is dense, the cementitious grout between it is not, and the grout is where most kitchen floor failures begin.
  • Three habits destroy a quarry tile floor faster than grease: acid on grout, caustic above pH 13 left to dry, and pressure above 1,200 psi aimed at joints.
  • A correct degreasing pass has five non-negotiable steps: dry removal, pre-spray, dwell, agitation, and wet-vacuum recovery of the dirty water before any rinse.
  • Degreasing frequency follows cooking volume, not calendar habit: light kitchens quarterly, medium-volume kitchens monthly, and fryer-heavy or 24-hour kitchens on a weekly full degrease.
  • Periodic machine degreasing in New Jersey typically runs $0.30 to $0.75 per square foot, restoration passes $1.50 to $3.00, and quarry tile replacement $12 to $25 per square foot installed, which is the number the floor care program is protecting.

A commercial kitchen floor is the only surface in the building that is walked on, greased, washed, dried, greased again and inspected, all in the same shift. Quarry tile has carried that load in American back-of-house kitchens for a century because it is hard, dimensionally stable, cheap to repair tile by tile and forgiving of heat.

It is not forgiving of the wrong chemistry.

The chronic complaint in restaurant floor care is not that the floor gets dirty. It is that the floor gets dirty faster every year, that the grout lines turn black within weeks of a deep clean, and that the only method that appears to work, a strong degreaser and a hard brush, seems to make the problem worse by the following quarter.

That pattern has a mechanical explanation, and it is the subject of this article. Grease does not attack quarry tile. It fills the pores and the grout, and every aggressive shortcut opens more pore and more grout to fill. The method that solves the problem is slower on the first visit and cheaper across three years, and it can be written down, scheduled and audited.

1. What is quarry tile and why does it behave differently in a commercial kitchen?

Quarry tile is an unglazed tile made from extruded natural clay, fired at high temperature and cut into squares or rectangles, most commonly 6 by 6 inches or 4 by 8 inches, at roughly half an inch thick. It has no glaze, which is the entire point: there is no decorative surface layer to chip, peel or wear through, so the color and texture run through the body of the tile.

That construction gives it three properties that matter in a kitchen. It is dimensionally stable under heat, so a fryer line does not lift it. It is hard enough to survive dropped pans, stepladders and the wheels of a rack. And it is repairable, because a single cracked tile can be lifted and replaced without touching its neighbors.

It also has a fourth property that the other three hide: it is porous. Unglazed clay absorbs water, typically in the range of 1% to 5% by weight depending on the body and the firing, which is low enough to feel solid underfoot and high enough to accept a grease film into the surface over time. The grout between the tiles is cementitious and far more absorbent than the tile itself, often several times more.

This is the first distinction a facility manager needs to hold. The tile and the grout are two different materials with two different tolerances, and almost every floor care method treats them as one.

The second distinction is between the tile face and the tile edge. The face is fired and dense. The edges and the joints are where water, grease and chemistry enter the assembly, and they are also the places where a brush or a pressure wand does the most damage per second of contact.

Quarry tile also sits in a floor assembly, not in isolation. Under the tile is a bedding layer, usually a cement mortar or a thin-set, and under that a substrate. Water that is not recovered from the surface does not disappear; it migrates into the grout, into the bedding and toward the drain, carrying emulsified grease with it. A floor that is "clean" and left wet is a floor that is being primed for the next grease load.

2. Why does grease penetrate quarry tile instead of sitting on top?

Cooking grease is not a single substance. It is a mixture of triglycerides, polymerized oils, carbonized protein, flour dust, starch and water, and its behavior changes with temperature. At 350°F in a fryer it is thin. At room temperature on a floor it becomes tacky, then semi-solid, then a varnish-like film as the unsaturated fats oxidize and polymerize.

That polymerization is the mechanism behind the black lines. Fresh grease is removable with warm water and a surfactant. Grease that has oxidized for two weeks has cross-linked into a film that water cannot wet, and that film bonds mechanically to any surface with an open pore.

Quarry tile offers two kinds of open pore. The tile face has microscopic surface porosity from the clay body, and the grout joint is a sponge by comparison. Grease enters both, and once inside it is protected from mops, from floor machines and from the casual spray-and-wipe that passes for degreasing in most kitchens.

There is a second mechanism that is often missed: grease is carried into the floor by water. When a crew mops with a dirty mop head and a bucket of gray water, it is not removing grease. It is distributing an emulsion across the floor and pushing it into the joints with every stroke. The water evaporates, the emulsion breaks, and the grease is deposited inside the grout, deeper than it was before.

This is why floors sometimes look worse after a mop than before it, and why the same crew can produce a clean dining room and a failing kitchen floor with the same technique. The dining room has sealed or glazed flooring and low grease load. The kitchen has an absorbent assembly and a heavy load, and the margin for a dirty mop is zero.

A third mechanism is thermal shock. Hot water opens the pores of the tile and the grout. Cold rinse water closes them abruptly. Grease that was suspended in the open pore gets trapped as the surface contracts. Kitchens that alternate very hot wash water and cold rinse water build a subsurface grease layer that no surface scrub will reach.

4. Which chemicals are safe for quarry tile floors?

The safe chemistry set for quarry tile is narrow, and it is defined by pH and by contact time rather than by brand.

Three rules govern this table.

Rule one: match the chemistry to the soil, not to the schedule. A neutral detergent on oxidized grease does nothing, and a heavy caustic on fresh grease is overkill that costs grout. The crew needs a decision rule, not a single jug.

Rule two: dwell time replaces pressure. Chemistry needs contact time to saponify fat. Five minutes of dwell with a pH 11 degreaser removes more grease than thirty seconds of scrubbing with the same product, and it removes it without touching the grout.

Rule three: never let the product dry. Dried chemistry concentrates, bonds to the tile and leaves a film that attracts soil. A section that cannot be completed inside the dwell window should not have been sprayed.

Dilution matters as much as product selection. Most alkaline degreasers are designed for a 1:20 to 1:64 dilution for floor work, and crews that pour concentrate directly on the floor are usually trying to compensate for missing dwell time. The result is a floor that smells of chemistry, feels slick and fails inspection on residue rather than on grease.

5. Which chemicals should never touch a quarry tile kitchen floor?

Five product families should be treated as prohibited on a quarry tile floor, and the reason in each case is the same: they remove the floor's ability to be cleaned.

Hydrochloric acid and muriatic acid. These are sometimes used to strip heavy mineral deposits or to "brighten" tile. On a cementitious grout they dissolve the binder. A single application at working strength can open joints permanently, and the damage is invisible until the grout begins to crumble weeks later.

Concentrated caustic soda above pH 13. Sodium hydroxide at high concentration and elevated temperature attacks the grout matrix and can leave a silicate film on the tile face. It is a legitimate product for hood and oven cleaning, where it contacts stainless and carbon steel, and it has no place on a tiled floor.

Solvent-based degreasers. Xylene, toluene and heavy solvent blends dissolve grease quickly and also dissolve the tile sealer, the adhesive in some repair patches and the finish on adjacent equipment. They evaporate into a closed kitchen and create a flammable atmosphere near pilot lights.

Bleach as a degreaser. Sodium hypochlorite is an oxidizer, not a surfactant. It does not remove grease, it bleaches the grout, and it reacts with ammonia-based products to produce chloramine gas. In a kitchen with a floor drain and a mop bucket, that reaction has happened more often than the industry likes to admit.

Acid-based bowl and descaler products used broadly. A grout-safe acid at pH 2 to 4 has a legitimate spot role on efflorescence and mineral scale. Broadcast across a floor, it is a grout removal program with a pleasant scent.

The commercial argument for prohibition is simple. A floor that is chemically damaged cannot be restored by cleaning; it can only be regrouted or replaced, and the cost of regrouting a 2,000 square foot kitchen runs several times the annual cost of the cleaning contract that caused the damage.

6. How do you degrease a quarry tile floor step by step?

The method is sequential, and the sequence is what protects the grout. Skipping a step does not save time; it moves the work into the next visit at a higher cost.

Step 1: remove dry soil first. Dust mop or sweep the entire area before any liquid touches it. Flour, breadcrumbs and fryer debris become a paste under water and are then pushed into the joints by the brush. Dry removal takes five minutes on 1,000 square feet and saves an entire rinse pass.

Step 2: pre-spray in sections. Work in sections of roughly 150 to 250 square feet, or four to six tiles wide along a wall. Apply the alkaline degreaser at the specified dilution with a low-pressure sprayer or a mop, from the farthest corner toward the drain. Never spray the whole floor at once; the far end will dry before the crew arrives.

Step 3: honor the dwell. Five to ten minutes for a standard degreaser, ten to fifteen for oxidized grease. Use the dwell to detail the adjacent stainless, the floor drain ring and the coved base. If the product begins to dry at the edges, re-wet it; do not add more concentrate.

Step 4: agitate mechanically, not violently. A deck brush with medium-stiff bristles or a floor machine with a red or blue pad at low speed. The goal is to lift the emulsified film, not to abrade the tile. Brush along the grout lines at the end of the pass, with light pressure, and never with a wire brush.

Step 5: recover the water before rinsing. This is the step most crews skip and the one that determines whether the job worked. Use a wet vacuum or a squeegee and wet vac to pull the dirty emulsion off the floor. Rinsing before recovery dilutes the grease and spreads it back across the surface.

Step 6: rinse twice with clean water. Two passes with clean water at 120°F to 140°F, recovering between passes. The first rinse removes the bulk of the residue, the second removes the film. A pH strip on the floor after the second rinse should read close to neutral.

Step 7: dry and inspect. Dry the floor with a squeegee or air mover before service. Walk the section with a flashlight held at a low angle; raking light shows grease film, haze and missed joints that look invisible under overhead light.

7. How do you treat grout without destroying it?

Grout is the part of the floor that decides how long the floor lasts, and it needs a different method from the tile.

Cementitious grout is a mixture of cement, sand and pigment. It is alkaline by nature, porous by design and mechanically weak compared with the tile. It responds well to alkaline cleaning and badly to acid, which is the opposite of what most descalers are formulated to do.

The workable grout protocol has four parts.

Agitate with the right brush. A soft-to-medium grout brush, sized to the joint, worked along the line rather than across it. Stiff brushes and wire brushes remove grout along with the soil, and the loss is permanent.

Use chemistry that matches the joint. An alkaline degreaser at pH 10 to 12 for grease, an enzyme digester for organic odor, and a grout-safe acid only for mineral bloom, applied with a bottle and rinsed within three minutes. Never a mop, never a broadcast spray.

Control the water. Grout cleaning should be done with minimal water and immediate recovery. A wet grout joint that is left to dry slowly wicks dissolved salts to the surface and produces the white bloom that crews then try to remove with more acid.

Restore the joint, do not just clean it. Grout that has receded, cracked or crumbled needs regrouting before it is sealed. Sealing a failing joint traps moisture and produces a joint that looks sealed and behaves worse. The decision framework between cleaning, sealing and full restoration is set out in the grout restoration pricing guide /floor-grout-restoration-pricing-guide, and the threshold is usually visible joint loss of more than about 2 millimeters.

Two numbers are worth remembering. A grout joint that is properly sealed and correctly cleaned should hold its color for six to twelve months in a medium-volume kitchen. A joint that is cleaned with acid every month will lose its surface within a year, and no sealer will bond to it.

Frequency follows cooking volume and menu type, not a fixed calendar. The same square footage in a salad prep kitchen and a wing restaurant carries completely different grease loads.

The table assumes a floor with intact grout and a functioning drain. A floor with open joints behaves like a high-volume floor regardless of cover count, because the grease load is going into the assembly rather than sitting on it.

Three practical adjustments matter more than the table.

Fryer proximity beats cover count. The three meters around a fryer, a grill or a wok station accumulate more polymerized grease than the entire rest of the kitchen. Zones should be defined by equipment, not by an even grid, and the fryer lane should be on a shorter cycle than the walk-in aisle.

Nightly mopping is not degreasing. A nightly mop with a neutral detergent removes fresh soil and keeps the floor presentable. It does not remove the oxidized film, and a program that relies on nightly mopping alone will need a restoration pass within eighteen to twenty-four months.

Seasonality is real. Summer menus, patio service and higher fryer volume in the fourth quarter all shift the load. A frequency set in February and never reviewed will be wrong by July.

For operations that clean after close, the floor pass has to fit inside the same window as the rest of the overnight restaurant cleaning /overnight-restaurant-cleaning scope, and the sequence should place floor degreasing before the final rinse of the kitchen so that water from equipment cleaning does not undo the work.

9. How do you keep a quarry tile floor from becoming a slip hazard?

Grease on quarry tile is a friction problem before it is an appearance problem, and the two are not the same measurement.

Clean, dry quarry tile has excellent slip resistance because of its textured, unglazed face. Clean, wet quarry tile is still good. Tile with a grease film, or tile with a polymer residue left by an improperly rinsed degreaser, can be dangerously slick even when it looks clean, because the film fills the texture that provides friction.

The reference point used in floor specification is the wet dynamic coefficient of friction, and the threshold commonly referenced for level interior floors is 0.42 under ANSI A326.3. A kitchen floor does not need a laboratory test to be managed, but it does need three controls.

Control one: rinse to neutral. Every degreasing pass ends with a pH check. A floor left at pH 10 or above has a soap film that behaves like a lubricant. Two clean-water rinses with recovery between them are the difference between a floor that is clean and a floor that is clean and safe.

Control two: recover water, do not push it. Squeegees that push water toward a drain leave a thin film across the whole path. Wet vacuums remove it. In a kitchen with a floor drain in the middle of the room, the squeegee path should end at the vacuum, not at the drain.

Control three: manage the transition zones. Most slip incidents in kitchens happen at the boundary between the kitchen and the dining room, or at the walk-in threshold, where grease meets a different floor surface with different friction. Mats, a defined dry zone and a scheduled mop of the transition belong in the daily checklist.

Two additional habits reduce risk without cost. Keep the floor dry during service with a dedicated spill mop and a "wet floor" protocol that is actually used, and inspect the floor at close with raking light, because a grease film that is invisible under overhead lighting is obvious at a low angle.

There is also a drainage dimension. Water that carries grease into the floor drain loads the grease interceptor, and a kitchen that washes floors heavily without a pre-scrape and recovery step will pump its interceptor more often. The relationship between floor washing and grease trap maintenance in New Jersey /restaurant-grease-trap-cleaning-nj is direct, and it is one of the few places where floor care shows up on a utility bill.

11. How do you handle black grease lines, yellowing and embedded soil?

The three chronic complaints about quarry tile have three different causes, and treating them with the same product is why they keep coming back.

Black lines along grout joints. This is polymerized grease plus carbon, bonded into the cementitious joint. It is removed by alkaline gel with a longer dwell, a soft grout brush and mechanical action along the joint, followed by recovery. It is not removed by more mopping, and it is not removed by acid, which etches the joint and gives the carbon a rougher surface to bond to next time.

Yellow or brown discoloration across the tile face. This is usually an oxidized grease film that has been spread by mopping rather than removed. It responds to a heavy-duty alkaline degreaser at pH 11 to 12 with a ten to fifteen minute dwell and a floor machine with a red pad. If the discoloration remains after two passes, the tile has absorbed the film and needs a restoration pass with extended dwell, not a stronger chemical.

Embedded soil that reappears within days. This is the signature of grease that has entered the bedding layer through open joints. Surface cleaning removes the visible layer, the floor dries, and the grease wicks back up. The only durable fix is to regrout the failed joints, clean the assembly and seal, which is why joint inspection belongs in every quarterly walk.

There is a fourth pattern worth naming because it is often misdiagnosed: a white or gray haze that appears after cleaning. This is not soil. It is mineral residue from hard water, dried chemistry or efflorescence drawn up from the substrate. It is treated with a grout-safe acid spot and a rinse, and it gets worse every time someone tries to scrub it off with more alkaline product.

The diagnostic sequence for any of these complaints is the same: identify the layer, test a one-square-meter patch with the mildest chemistry that could work, measure the result under raking light, and only then commit the crew to the full area. Testing on a patch costs ten minutes and prevents a night of work in the wrong direction.

12. How do you seal quarry tile and grout, and does it work?

Sealing is the part of quarry tile care most often sold and least often explained, and the explanation matters because the wrong sealer makes the floor worse.

There are two families. Topical sealers form a film on the surface: acrylic, urethane or epoxy coatings. Penetrating sealers soak into the pore and line it with a hydrophobic molecule, usually a silane or siloxane, without changing the surface appearance.

For a commercial kitchen floor, topical sealers are a poor fit. Grease, heat, fryer splash and daily mopping attack the film, which then peels in sheets, traps moisture underneath and creates a surface that is both ugly and slippery. A peeling topical film on a kitchen floor is worse than no sealer at all.

Penetrating sealers are the appropriate choice. They reduce water and grease absorption without creating a film, they do not change the slip characteristics of the tile face, and they can be reapplied. They do not make the floor grease-proof; they buy time between cleanings and slow the rate at which soil enters the joint.

Three conditions determine whether sealing works.

The floor must be clean and dry before application. Sealer applied over residual grease or damp grout will not bond, and the failure is invisible until the first degrease. Moisture content should be checked with a meter on the grout, not assumed from the surface.

The grout must be sound. Sealing a crumbled joint seals nothing. Regrout first, cure, then seal.

The sealer must be maintained. A penetrating sealer in a medium-volume kitchen typically needs reapplication every twelve to twenty-four months, and the interval shortens with heavy fryer load and aggressive chemistry. The maintenance test is simple: bead a few drops of water on the joint. If it soaks in within a minute, the sealer is spent.

Sealing is also the point where the conversation moves from cleaning to asset protection. The service comparison between sealing and full restoration, including price bands and expected life, is covered in the commercial tile and grout deep cleaning /tile-grout-deep-cleaning-commercial reference.

13. What equipment does a quarry tile degreasing program need?

The equipment list for quarry tile is shorter than most proposals suggest, and the specific models matter less than the capabilities.

A wet vacuum with a squeegee head. This is the single most important piece of equipment in the program. Without recovery, the crew is redistributing grease. A wet vac with a 15-gallon tank and a front-mount squeegee handles a 1,500 square foot kitchen in two fills.

A floor machine at low speed with the right pads. A 17-inch or 20-inch machine at 175 to 300 rpm with red or blue pads for general degreasing, and a grout brush attachment for joints. High-speed burnishers belong in retail, not in a greasy kitchen.

A deck brush and a hand grout brush. The deck brush for open floor, the hand brush for joints along walls, under equipment and inside coved corners. Medium-stiff bristles, never wire.

A low-pressure sprayer with a fan tip. For even application at the correct dilution. A pump sprayer is adequate; a hose-end proportioner is better for large floors because it removes the mixing error.

A two-bucket system or a clean-water source. Rinse water must be clean. A single bucket of rinse water becomes gray water after one section.

A pH meter or strips and a moisture meter. The first verifies rinsing, the second verifies readiness for sealer. Both cost less than a single service call and settle arguments.

Air movers. Drying the floor before service is part of the job, not an optional finish.

What the list deliberately excludes: pressure washers above 1,200 psi, wire brushes, steam machines used at the joint, and any tool that removes material rather than soil. Those tools produce a visible result on the first visit and a measurable loss of grout by the fourth.

Equipment also determines crew time. A two-person crew with a wet vac, a floor machine and a sprayer can degrease 1,500 square feet of kitchen floor in roughly 2.5 to 3.5 hours including setup, recovery and rinse. The same job with mops and buckets takes longer and produces a worse result, which is the practical argument for the equipment list.

14. How do you document quarry tile care for health and fire inspections?

Documentation is what separates a floor care program from a floor care habit, and it is the part that survives staff turnover.

The documentation set has four components.

A floor zone map. A simple plan of the kitchen divided into zones, with square footage and the assigned frequency for each. The map ends the argument about whether the fryer lane was supposed to be done on Tuesday.

A signed cleaning log per zone per visit. Date, zone, chemistry used, dilution, dwell time, crew initials. One line per zone is enough. The log is the evidence that the frequency in the program was actually delivered.

A quarterly condition report. Photographs of the same four or five fixed locations every quarter, plus a joint condition rating and a sealer bead test. This is the document that shows a trend, and a trend is what justifies a restoration pass before the floor fails.

Chemical records. Safety data sheets on site, dilution charts posted, and a record of which products are approved for grout and which are prohibited. In an inspection, the presence of an acid product with no restriction on its use is a finding waiting to happen.

The regulatory frame is straightforward. The FDA Food Code and the New Jersey retail food rules at N.J.A.C. 8:24 require floors in food preparation areas to be clean, smooth, durable, nonabsorbent and easily cleanable, with coved base and proper drainage. A quarry tile floor with open, grease-filled joints fails the "easily cleanable" and "nonabsorbent" intent even if it was mopped an hour before the inspector arrived.

Fire inspections look at the floor for a different reason: grease accumulation on the floor near cooking equipment is part of the same housekeeping picture as hood and duct condition, and a floor program that documents degreasing frequency supports the broader grease management narrative that insurers and fire marshals expect.

The documentation should live with the cleaning contract, not in a binder in a manager's office. A program that can be produced in ninety seconds during an inspection is worth more than a better program that cannot be found.

15. When should a kitchen stop cleaning and restore the floor instead?

There is a point where cleaning stops being the answer, and it is defined by the condition of the assembly rather than by the appearance of the surface.

Five signals indicate that a restoration pass is due.

Joints have receded by more than 2 millimeters, or the grout crumbles under a fingernail. Cleaning cannot rebuild a joint. Regrout first, then clean, then seal.

Grease returns within 48 hours of a full degrease. The grease is in the bedding layer, not on the surface, and no surface chemistry will reach it.

Tiles sound hollow when tapped, or edges are lifting. Water has entered under the tile. A restoration pass will not fix a debonded tile, and the affected tiles should be reset or replaced before the floor is sealed.

The floor stays slick after two clean-water rinses and a pH check. The texture of the tile face has been worn or filled. Mechanical restoration with a diamond pad system, followed by a penetrating sealer, is the intervention.

The kitchen has failed a floor item on inspection twice in twelve months. This is a documentation and method problem that has become a compliance problem, and it needs a program rather than another night of scrubbing.

The economics are not close. A restoration pass at $1.50 to $3.00 per square foot on a 1,500 square foot kitchen costs $2,250 to $4,500 and buys several years. Replacement at $12 to $25 per square foot costs $18,000 to $37,500 plus downtime, lost covers and the disruption of a working kitchen. Regrouting at $4 to $9 per square foot is the middle path and is often the right one when the tile body is sound.

The decision sequence that keeps a program from oscillating between panic cleans is simple: measure the joint, test the sealer, check the return interval, then choose between clean, regrout and restore. A floor care program that follows that sequence for three years will spend less than a program that replaces the floor every eight.

FAQ

How often should a commercial kitchen quarry tile floor be degreased?

Follow cooking volume. Light kitchens under 40 covers a day need a quarterly full degrease, medium-volume kitchens with frying need a monthly one, and fryer-heavy or 24-hour kitchens need a weekly full degrease with grout detailing monthly.

Can I use bleach to clean a quarry tile kitchen floor?

No. Bleach is an oxidizer, not a degreaser, so it does not remove grease. It bleaches grout, degrades cementitious joints over time, and reacts with ammonia-based products to produce chloramine gas.

Is vinegar safe on quarry tile grout?

No. Vinegar is acidic and will dissolve the cement binder in the grout. A grout-safe acid at pH 2 to 4 has a narrow spot role on mineral scale, applied briefly and rinsed immediately, but vinegar is never a routine floor product.

What is the best way to remove black grease from grout lines?

Apply an alkaline gel at pH 11 to 12, allow ten to fifteen minutes of dwell without letting it dry, agitate along the joint with a soft-to-medium grout brush, then recover the water with a wet vacuum. Acid makes the joint rougher and the black line returns faster.

Does sealing quarry tile stop grease from sticking?

A penetrating sealer slows absorption and buys time between cleanings, but it does not make the floor grease-proof. Sealer must be reapplied every 12 to 24 months in a medium-volume kitchen, and sooner near fryer lines.

How much does quarry tile floor cleaning cost in New Jersey?

Periodic machine degreasing typically runs $0.30 to $0.75 per square foot, grout detailing $0.40 to $0.90, deep degrease and restoration $1.50 to $3.00, and penetrating sealer $0.60 to $1.40. Nightly floor care is usually bundled into the cleaning contract.

How long does it take to degrease a commercial kitchen floor?

A two-person crew with a wet vacuum, floor machine and sprayer handles about 1,500 square feet in 2.5 to 3.5 hours including setup, recovery and two rinse passes. Heavy restoration work on the same area runs a full overnight shift.

Can a pressure washer be used on quarry tile?

Only at low pressure and never aimed at the joints. Above roughly 1,200 psi the water erodes grout, drives moisture under the tile and causes debonding. Most kitchen floors are better served by a floor machine and a wet vacuum than by a pressure washer.

Why does my kitchen floor look dirty again two days after cleaning?

Grease is coming back from the bedding layer through open joints, or the rinse left a residue film that attracts soil. Check joint condition first, then verify the rinse pH, then test the sealer.

Do quarry tile floors need to be stripped and resealed like VCT?

No. Quarry tile is unglazed and does not carry a sacrificial floor finish, so there is nothing to strip. A penetrating sealer is used instead, and it does not build a film that peels.

Next step

If the floor returns to black within days of a deep clean, the problem is not the crew's effort. It is the method, the chemistry or the joint condition, and all three can be diagnosed in a single walk with a flashlight, a pH strip and a moisture meter.
