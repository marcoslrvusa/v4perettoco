NFPA 96 Explained: Hood Cleaning Frequencies and Documentation

Hood cleaning frequency requirements, documentation standards and what fire inspections verify

D15.528 palavras23 minQuery: NFPA 96 hood cleaning requirementsDestino: /restaurant-hood-exhaust-cleaning-nj

Direct answer: NFPA 96 sets fire protection requirements for commercial cooking operations, including exhaust hood cleaning at frequencies based on cooking volume. High-volume kitchens typically clean monthly, moderate kitchens quarterly, and light kitchens semiannually, with documentation required after each service and verified at the next inspection.

Key takeaways

  • The NFPA 96 cleaning frequency is set by cooking volume, not by a fixed calendar, with monthly, quarterly and semiannual intervals for high, moderate and light kitchens.
  • The frequency ladder runs from monthly cleaning for high-volume operations such as fryers and wok cooking to quarterly service for moderate kitchens and semiannual service for light menus.
  • NFPA 96 requires documented hood cleaning, and the documentation trail of logs, service reports, photos and filter records is what the fire inspector reviews.
  • Fire inspections verify hood cleanliness, suppression function and access to exhaust components, and failures usually trace back to missed intervals or missing records.
  • Hood cleaning should run on a schedule that respects operating hours, dry time and slip risk, so the kitchen stays compliant without closing for a deep clean every visit.

1. What is NFPA 96 and what does it cover for commercial kitchens?

NFPA 96 is the National Fire Protection Association standard known as Fire Protection Standard for Commercial Cooking Operations. The standard defines fire protection requirements for commercial cooking operations, covering the design, installation and maintenance of exhaust hood systems and the fire suppression that protects them. Within the NFPA family, NFPA 96 works alongside NFPA 13, which addresses the inspection, testing and maintenance of automatic suppression systems.

For a restaurant operator, the practical meaning is that the exhaust system is not a cosmetic element. It is an engineered fire protection component that removes heat, smoke and grease from the cooking area. A dirty hood reduces that removal capacity, and a suppression system that has not been inspected or serviced cannot be evaluated accurately.

The standard directs attention to cleaning frequency based on cooking volume. The enforceable interval is set by the authority having jurisdiction, which in New Jersey and New York includes the local fire marshal, the FDNY in New York City, and the health departments that coordinate with fire safety reviews. The operator responsibility is to maintain the hood according to the applicable requirement and to keep the documentation trail current.

NFPA 96 is not a restaurant marketing document. It is a referenced standard that operators, inspectors and insurers use to define the cleaning obligation. The obligation is operational, and the documentation is evidentiary. When the obligation is clear, the operator can schedule service with confidence, and when the documentation is clear, the operator can defend the schedule at inspection.

The standard also connects to the wider fire protection chain. The hood is the first line of defense for the cooking operation, and the suppression system is the second. A cleaning program that addresses only the hood, without the suppression schedule, leaves the second line of defense unmaintained. The operator should therefore run both threads in parallel, because a kitchen that cleans the hood on schedule and ignores the suppression inspection creates the same exposure as one that skips the hood entirely.

The obligation has a commercial consequence that operators often underestimate. Fire protection is priced into the contract through cleaning frequency, and the cleaning frequency is priced through the cooking volume. A restaurant that cannot state its band can neither quote the service accurately nor defend the invoice against an audit.

The standard does not describe a cleaning product or a vendor method. It describes an outcome: a cooking operation whose exhaust system performs under fire load. The cleaning frequency is the operational translation of that outcome, and the documentation is the proof of the translation. A restaurant that cannot state the outcome it protects has not yet built the program that protects it.

The standard is adopted by enforcement, not by preference. A kitchen that aligns its program with the standard before the first inspection is far less exposed than one that discovers the gap during the review. The relationship between the hood and the kitchen is also a relationship between the operator and the insurer, because insurers evaluate the cleaning program as part of the underwriting of fire liability, and the underwriting file is built from the cleaning records.

2. How often does NFPA 96 require commercial hood cleaning?

NFPA 96 ties the cleaning obligation to the volume of cooking. The intervals referenced in industry practice against the standard are organized in three bands, and the band an operation falls into determines the cleaning rhythm.

Cooking volumeCleaning intervalTypical operations
High-volumeMonthlyFryers, grills, wok cooking, open flame, 24-hour operation
Moderate-volumeQuarterlyFull-service kitchen with high-volume grill or fryer
Low-volume / lightSemiannualCafes, light menus, oven-based kitchens

The high-volume band is the most demanding. Fryer and wok cooking generate dense grease accumulation inside the hood and duct, and the fire risk rises with the frequency and intensity of service. Moderate kitchens, running full-service cooking at high volume several times per week, sit in the quarterly band. Light kitchens, with low-grease menus and predominantly oven or baking operations, sit at the semiannual reference.

These figures are typical operating ranges from the United States market, not a universal law. The authority having jurisdiction, the insurer and the landlord hold the final word, and restaurants should confirm the applicable requirement with the fire inspector before scheduling service.

The frequency band is not only a cleaning schedule decision. It is a procurement decision. A restaurant that knows it sits in the high-volume band can quote hood cleaning against a monthly service through a partner such as restaurant hood exhaust cleaning in New Jersey /restaurant-hood-exhaust-cleaning-nj, and it can evaluate a vendor on the depth of the cleaning scope.

The high-volume band carries the largest cleaning obligation in dollar terms per visit, because the cleaning scope itself scales with the grease load. Small systems in light kitchens can be cleaned with a shorter duct and a lower frequency, while large systems with long duct runs and rooftop fans require the most labor per service. The moderate band sits between the two, and the decision to clean quarterly rather than monthly usually comes down to menu intensity rather than square footage.

The operator should also account for the number of cooking lines. A kitchen with a fryer line, a grill line and a wok line carries a higher combined grease load than a kitchen with a single line, and the combined obligation is additive rather than substitutive.

The obligation scales with the grease load, and the grease load scales with the cooking lines, the menu and the service hours. The operator should therefore size the cleaning frequency to the operation rather than to the square footage of the kitchen, because the variable that drives the risk is not the room size but the fire load created by the cooking process itself.

The operator should also review the cleaning scope at the same time. A fryer line, a grill line and a wok line require a cleaning sequence that addresses each line, because the grease load is not shared across lines and the cleaning must be additive across the whole system. The sequence should be written before the service, not improvised during it.

3. How is the NFPA 96 cleaning frequency set by cooking volume?

The cleaning frequency is a function of cooking volume, and cooking volume is a function of throughput, menu intensity and equipment type. Three drivers move the interval up or down.

The first driver is the cooking equipment class. Open flame, solid fuel, wok and high-heat fryer operations accumulate grease faster than moderate-heat grilling or baking. The second driver is the meal pattern. Restaurants that run multiple seatings and high ticket volume per night clean more often than single-meal operations. The third driver is the presence of solid fuel equipment, which adds a fuel-related fire load that pushes the obligation toward the high-volume band.

A kitchen that sits near the boundary between two bands should document the reasoning for the interval it adopts, because that reasoning becomes part of the compliance record. A defensible kitchen maintains the interval, keeps the records and can show the inspector the basis for the schedule.

The frequency also moves with the menu. A restaurant that changes its menu, adds a fryer or opens a late-night service should revisit the band. The NFPA 96 obligation is tied to the operation as it exists, and an operation that changes without revisiting the obligation creates a gap between the cleaning program and the fire risk.

The operators should also track meal period intensity. A kitchen that runs a single high-volume dinner service has a different obligation than one that runs two or three seatings at high volume, and the cleaning frequency should reflect that difference rather than relying on the headline band alone.

The operator should also reconcile the cleaning frequency with the cleaning scope. A high-volume kitchen that cleans monthly needs a deeper cleaning method than a light kitchen that cleans semiannually, and the method should follow the band rather than the calendar alone.

The operator should also publish the risk ranking of the menu items, because the ranking helps the cleaning team understand why a high-volume item drives the frequency. The ranking is a practical tool for the cleaning team and for the supervisor who runs the inspection, and it should be part of the written program.

4. How does the hood cleaning interval relate to the kitchen deep clean?

Hood cleaning is one layer of the kitchen deep clean, and the two are frequently confused. Hood cleaning addresses the exhaust system, the grease lanes and the surrounding surfaces that channel combustion byproducts. Kitchen deep cleaning addresses floors, walls, equipment exteriors, shelving and trash areas.

The relationship between the two matters because a deep clean that does not include the hood leaves the fire risk unaddressed, while a hood cleaning that runs without the degrease of the kitchen floor leaves the slip risk and the grease lane unaddressed. In practice, the hood interval and the deep clean cadence should be scheduled so that the hood is cleaned within the required band and the kitchen reaches a documented clean state before service resumes.

Deep cleaning programs for restaurants usually run a nightly or weekly kitchen degrease, a periodic deep clean, and a periodic hood service. The deep clean cadence and the hood interval are not the same number, and conflating them produces either over-cleaning or under-cleaning. Over-cleaning spends labor and budget without adding protection. Under-cleaning leaves the fire obligation unmet.

For operations that need the full protection chain, a partner that runs both the kitchen deep clean and the hood service is the practical route, as described in overnight restaurant cleaning /overnight-restaurant-cleaning.

The sequencing matters because the degrease and the hood cleaning are not interchangeable. The degrease prepares the surface for the extraction and for the inspection, and the hood cleaning addresses the exhaust. Running them out of sequence leaves one obligation unmet while the other is addressed.

The operator should also document the cleaning sequence in the deep clean program, so that the hood cleaning step is not improvised on the day of service and the degrease is not skipped because the hood cleaning took priority.

The operator should also define the handoff between the deep clean and the hood service, because the two are often performed by different teams and the documentation should follow both. The handoff should be written, not assumed, and it should be reviewed before the first deep clean that includes a hood step.

5. What documentation does NFPA 96 require after a hood cleaning service?

After a hood cleaning service, the operator should keep a documentation trail that supports the cleaning obligation and defends the schedule at inspection. The core records are the cleaning log, the service report, before and after photos, and the filter or grease record.

Documentation itemWhat it proves
Cleaning logDate, technician, scope and outcome of the hood cleaning
Service reportMethods and products used during the cleaning
Before and after photosCondition before and after the cleaning
Filter recordCleaning or replacement of hood filters

The cleaning log records the date, the technician, the scope of work and the outcome. The service report captures the methods and products used. Before and after photos provide visual evidence of the condition before and after the service. The filter record tracks filter cleaning or replacement. Together, these records form the documentation trail that NFPA 96 expects the operator to maintain.

The documentation should be specific enough to be reviewed. A record that says hood cleaned is not the same as a record that says hood cleaned with degreasing of the exhaust and a verified inspection. The standard and the inspector look for evidence of the obligation being met, and specificity is what converts a cleaning event into a defensible compliance event.

The documentation trail should be continuous rather than assembled from separate documents. The operator should keep the service report with the cleaning log, so the evidence reads as one event rather than two separate records that were reconciled later.

The cost side of the obligation also deserves attention. The commercial cleaning cost pricing guide for New Jersey /insights/commercial-cleaning-cost-nj-pricing-guide-2026 walks through the unit economics of commercial cleaning, and the hood service is usually priced as a line item inside that larger program rather than as a standalone fee.

The operator should keep the cleaning log and the service report in a single record, with the same date and the same scope, so that the inspector sees one continuous obligation rather than two documents that may not align.

The operator should also define the retention period for each record, because the inspector and the insurer may ask for records that are older than the cleaning interval. The retention policy should be part of the program and should be applied consistently.

6. What records must a restaurant keep for the fire inspector?

The records that a restaurant should keep for the fire inspector are the cleaning logs, the hood service reports, the suppression inspection records, the filter logs and any before and after photography. The inspector reviews these records to confirm that the cleaning intervals were met and that the maintenance program is current.

Restaurants that keep the records in one place, such as a folder at the host stand or in the back office, can present them quickly during an inspection. The quality of the record matters as much as the cleaning itself, because a complete record turns a cleaning event into a defensible compliance event.

The suppression inspection records deserve separate attention. NFPA 96 and NFPA 13 both call for periodic inspection of the suppression system, and the cleaning program and the suppression program are evaluated together during the fire review. An operator that runs a clean hood cleaning program but has an overdue suppression inspection is not compliant, because the protection chain is only as strong as its weakest scheduled element.

The records should be retained for the period required by the jurisdiction and by the insurer, and the retention policy should be documented. A restaurant that cannot produce the records at inspection has a documentation failure even when the cleaning was performed correctly.

The operator should assign a retention schedule and a responsible person for the records, because records without an owner tend to drift and to be incomplete when the review happens.

The operator should also confirm the insurer requirements for the records, because the insurer may require a specific format or a specific period. The operator should reconcile the insurer requirements with the authority requirements before the inspection, and the reconciliation should be documented.

7. What does the fire inspection actually verify about the hood?

The fire inspection verifies the condition of the hood system, the function of the suppression, and the accessibility of the exhaust components. The inspector reviews the cleaning evidence, checks the grease accumulation, and confirms that the suppression system has been inspected and maintained within its own schedule.

The verification is not limited to the hood interior. The inspector may review the surrounding surfaces, the grease lanes, the filters and the clearance around the equipment. Restaurants should prepare the documentation trail before the inspection, because the inspection outcome is usually a function of what the inspector finds and what the operator can show.

The inspection also has a practical dimension. A hood that cannot be accessed for service cannot be cleaned within the required interval, and an inspection that cannot be performed cannot produce the evidence the operator needs. Access planning, including clearances and any rooftop exhaust components, is therefore part of the compliance program rather than an afterthought.

The operator should run the cleaning evidence through a checklist before the inspection, so that the inspector is not reviewing an assembled narrative but a prepared file.

The operator should also prepare the suppression test records, because the suppression function is evaluated alongside the hood and the failure signal moves the obligation to the suppression program. The operator should not treat the two as independent, because the protection chain is only as strong as its weakest scheduled element.

8. How do signage and access affect NFPA 96 compliance?

Signage and access are part of the compliance picture. The hood and the exhaust system need clear access for inspection, and the operation needs the signage and labeling that the standard and the authority having jurisdiction expect. Restricted access, missing clearances and unlabeled equipment can turn a routine inspection into a failed one.

Access also affects the cleaning schedule. A hood that cannot be reached for service cannot be cleaned within the required interval, and a cleaning that cannot be performed within operating hours requires coordination with the kitchen schedule.

The practical fix is a written access plan. The plan names who can access the hood, when, and under what conditions. The plan should be consistent with the cleaning schedule and with the inspection requirements, because the three are evaluated together during the fire review.

The access plan should be reviewed whenever the hood configuration changes, whether through equipment replacement or kitchen reconfiguration. A reconfiguration that removes a clearance or adds a duct run changes the cleaning and inspection obligations, and the operator should update the records accordingly.

The operator should also confirm that the signage reflects the current cooking volume band, because outdated signage creates confusion between the written standard and the applied standard.

The operator should also coordinate the signage review with the cleaning schedule, because the signage should reflect the current band and the cleaning should follow the signage. The two should be reviewed together, and the review should produce a written outcome.

9. What mistakes cause NFPA 96 inspection failures?

The most common failures trace back to missed cleaning intervals, missing documentation and cleaning that does not match the required scope. An interval that is skipped under scheduling pressure is the first failure, and the absence of a documentation trail is the second. A cleaning that addresses the exterior but leaves the duct interior untreated is a third failure mode.

Restaurants that keep the interval in a written schedule, execute the cleaning within the band and keep the records current rarely fail for the hood itself. The failures that remain usually involve access, training of the cleaning team and the coordination between cleaning and kitchen operations.

A fourth failure mode deserves separate mention. Cleaning that runs during operating hours, without the dry time and slip protection that the operation requires, can leave the kitchen in a condition that the inspector reads as a cleaning failure. The timing of the cleaning matters as much as the cleaning itself, and the operation should sequence hood cleaning so that the kitchen is not put back into service before the surface is safe.

The pattern is consistent across enforcement agencies: the failures that reach the inspector are rarely novel, and they are usually the predictable result of an interval that slipped, a record that was not kept, or a cleaning that was under-scoped.

The operator should treat the cleaning schedule as a controlled document, with version control and review, because the schedule is the instrument that converts the obligation into action.

The operator should also train the cleaning team on the interval and the documentation, because a trained team executes the interval and fills the records. The training is part of the program and should be refreshed when the band changes.

10. How should a kitchen schedule hood cleaning around operating hours?

Hood cleaning should be scheduled to avoid recontamination and to respect the operating schedule. Deep cleaning and degreasing that runs during service can leave slip risk and recontamination, and the standard expects the hood to be cleaned at a time that supports safe operation.

Overnight cleaning windows are the practical solution for many restaurants, because the kitchen is closed and the dry time can be managed. Scheduling should also account for the dry time of any degreasing agents and for the need to verify the hood condition before the next service period.

The schedule should also account for the fact that the cleaning team and the kitchen team may not share the same shift. Restaurants that run their own cleaning staff should align the hood cleaning with the kitchen close, and restaurants that use an outside vendor should give the vendor the operating calendar rather than the cleaning calendar.

The cleaning provider should be able to show the schedule against the cooking volume band, and the operator should keep the records of each cleaning so that the interval is defensible at inspection.

The operator should build the cleaning into the operating rhythm, with a standing cleaning day or a standing overnight window, rather than treating cleaning as an emergency response.

The operator should also schedule the cleaning in the closed period and verify the dry time before service, because the inspection follows service and the service follows the cleaning.

The operator should also build a cleaning checklist into the overnight window, so that the cleaning is executed against a written list and the records are filled from the same source. The checklist is the bridge between execution and evidence, and it should be signed by the cleaning team.

11. How does hood cleaning relate to the kitchen deep cleaning program?

Hood cleaning belongs to the kitchen deep cleaning program, but it operates on its own interval. The deep clean cadence may be weekly or monthly for some operations, while the hood interval may be monthly or quarterly. The operator should map the hood cleaning onto the deep clean calendar rather than treating the two as interchangeable.

A kitchen deep clean that includes a hood cleaning step at the right interval satisfies both the cleaning obligation and the deep clean program, provided the documentation trail is updated for both scopes. A deep clean that skips the hood step leaves the fire obligation to be handled by a separate service, and the two should not be run as parallel but unsynchronized processes.

The operator should also separate the degrease from the extraction, because the two belong to different layers and the documentation should reflect both. The restaurant commercial cleaning services /restaurant-commercial-cleaning page maps the full scope of the deep clean program for restaurants.

The deep clean program should publish a cleaning calendar that shows the hood service within the required band and the deep clean cadence in the same rhythm, so the operator can see both obligations on one view.

The operator should map the deep clean calendar and the hood interval on the same planning tool, because two calendars produce two sources of truth.

The operator should also align the deep clean program's approval process with the hood cleaning approval process, because the two feed the same compliance narrative. The approval should be documented and the documentation should be kept with the cleaning records.

12. What should a restaurant checklist include before an NFPA 96 inspection?

Before an NFPA 96 inspection, a restaurant should run a checklist that covers the hood condition, the cleaning evidence, the documentation trail, the suppression records and the access requirements. The checklist should confirm that the cleaning interval was met, that the records are complete and that the suppression system is current.

The checklist should be run as a live document before the inspection, not assembled after. The live checklist lets the operator correct gaps while they are still small, and it produces a record of what was checked, when and by whom.

The operator should also confirm the provider scope before the inspection. Cleaning exteriors and adjacent surfaces is one scope, while full hood and duct cleaning is another, and the operator should know which scope is active before the evidence is reviewed through a partner such as commercial kitchen cleaning in New Jersey /commercial-kitchen-cleaning-nj.

The checklist should be usable by the cleaning team and by the manager, with the same items, so that the cleaning execution and the inspection evidence come from the same standard.

The operator should run the checklist before the inspection and archive the result, so that the checklist becomes a repeatable compliance artifact.

The operator should also confirm the fire inspector's checklist before the inspection, so that the operator's checklist and the inspector's checklist are reconciled. The reconciliation prevents gaps, and the operator should run the final version on the day of the inspection.

13. When should a restaurant schedule hood cleaning before or after a deep clean?

The timing of hood cleaning relative to a deep clean depends on the state of the kitchen. A deep clean that includes degreasing should be sequenced so that the hood cleaning and the kitchen degrease support each other rather than conflict. Scheduling the hood cleaning after a deep clean can leave the hood uncleared if the deep clean did not include a hood scope, and scheduling it before can leave the kitchen dirty if the hood cleaning is followed by floor work without degrease.

The operator should schedule the hood cleaning as part of the deep clean sequence, with the degrease preceding the hood cleaning when grease is the driver, and with the documentation trail updated at the point of service.

The operator should also plan the hood cleaning relative to the operating calendar, so that the deep clean does not push the hood service past the required interval. The two schedules live in different systems, and the operator is responsible for keeping them aligned.

The operator should time the hood cleaning before the deep clean when the deep clean is degrease-led, and after when the deep clean is extraction-led, and keep the records aligned with the cleaning event.

The operator should also set a review date for the deep clean program and the hood cleaning program, because both are living documents that should be reviewed on a fixed cadence. The review should produce a record of changes and should update the cleaning schedule.

14. How does the cooking volume band affect the cleaning scope?

The cooking volume band determines more than the frequency. It determines the cleaning method, the products used, the equipment required and the inspection standard. A high-volume kitchen needs a cleaning method that addresses dense grease, while a light kitchen can use a lighter method within the semiannual band.

The operator should also benchmark the cleaning cost against the band, because the band determines the frequency and the frequency determines the cost. A high-volume kitchen in the monthly band will have a higher cleaning cost than a light kitchen in the semiannual band, and the operator should be able to defend both numbers. The benchmark should be part of the proposal and the proposal should be part of the program.

The operator should also publish the cleaning cost ranges by band, so that the proposal can be compared with the cleaning program. The ranges should be written in the same format as the cleaning frequency, because the two are evaluated together.

15. What is the relationship between NFPA 96 and the health department inspection?

NFPA 96 and the health department inspection are related but distinct obligations. The health department reviews sanitation and food safety, while the fire authority reviews fire protection. Both inspections can reference the same cleaning evidence, and both can fail for the same interval gaps.

The operator should therefore prepare the cleaning evidence once and use it for both reviews, because the evidence is the same and the requirement is the same. A kitchen that keeps the records and the interval aligned can answer both inspections from one file.

The operator should also confirm the fire authority's inspection protocol, because the protocol defines what the inspector will check and what evidence will be required. The operator should align its program to the protocol before the inspection, because alignment reduces friction and failure risk.

The operator should also keep a copy of the protocol requirement in the cleaning file, so that the cleaning evidence and the protocol requirement are directly comparable during the inspection. The comparison is the audit. The operator should also train the inspection team on the protocol requirement, because the comparison only works when both sides are read with the same standard.
