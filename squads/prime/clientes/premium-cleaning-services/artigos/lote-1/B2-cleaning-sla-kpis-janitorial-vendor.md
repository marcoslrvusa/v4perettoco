> **Direct answer:** A cleaning service level agreement should be backed by five measurable cleaning KPIs — completion, rework, inspection score, response time and frequency adherence — defined against a documented inspection methodology and reported weekly, turning the question of whether a vendor is clean into a measurable, contractable standard.

## Key takeaways

- Replace subjective “looks clean” reviews with scored inspections and a written rework definition before any SLA target is set.
- Anchor the first SLA targets to a measured baseline, not to a vendor promise of speed.
- Keep the rework rate under 8 percent as a working threshold before debating completion targets.
- Tie contractual consequences, credit or re-clean window, to specific missed KPIs instead of overall satisfaction.
- Report the same five KPIs every week so trends become decisions, not anecdotes.

## 1. Why is “looks clean” not a KPI a janitorial vendor contract can enforce?

A cleaning contract that measures quality with the phrase “looks clean” leaves the most important variable in the building without a number.

The facility manager holds a standard in their head. The vendor holds one in its procedure. The conversation between the two happens in the gap, and the gap is where complaints, renegotiation and blame live.

“Looks clean” is a perception-based standard. It can be recalled by a guest, a manager or an inspector, but it cannot be scheduled, measured or enforced. When the crew cleans to a perception standard, the client pays for variable outcomes across visits. When the client evaluates to a perception standard, the crew has no target to hit between inspections.

The first move in a cleaning SLA program is to translate the standard into something measurable. The translation has three steps. Define the tasks. Define the inspection criteria. Assign a score.

Once the criteria are written, the cleaning KPIs can follow. Without a written criteria list, an inspection becomes a feeling. With a written list, inspection becomes a measurement, and a measurement becomes something that can be contracted, priced, reported and defended.

Industry benchmarks in commercial cleaning are framed as typical operating ranges, not as laws. The rework rates and score distributions that follow are common practice in the United States market, and they describe what well-run cleaning operations look like when supervision and inspection are documented.

When a contract skips this translation, the SLA has no teeth. The agreement can still be enforced through access, supervision and inspection, but those are control mechanisms. Control mechanisms keep a crew on the site. KPIs tell the contract what the crew is being measured against.

An SLA written before the criteria exist is an SLA written on air. The facility manager who drafts the cleaning standard first, in observable terms, and then asks the vendor to meet it is the manager who can later enforce it. The reverse order produces a contract that reads well and fails often, and the failure is always attributed to the vendor even when the standard was never testable.

The cost of that order is usually paid in the first month of the contract. Small variances that a written standard would have absorbed become misses, and misses without a measurement behind them become negotiations without a floor.

The inspection layer and the criteria list should be treated as one artifact. A criteria list without an inspection process produces scores that no one can reproduce. An inspection process without a written criteria list produces scores that no one can defend. The cleaning program that names both, and keeps both current, is the program that can eventually write an enforceable SLA.

The calibration step should be scheduled before the scorecard goes live, because uncalibrated inspectors produce uncalibrated scores, and the SLA then inherits the error. A two-inspector comparison during the first month is enough to establish a baseline for the scoring process, after which the monthly manager audit takes over.

A cleaning program that has never run a calibration will produce scores that vary more than the cleaning itself varies. The variance is usually small in the first weeks and then stabilizes, but the SLA should not be written against an unstable scoring process. The first two months should be treated as a calibration period, with the scorecard reviewed weekly and the scoring rules adjusted until the inter-inspector agreement is stable.

The calibration should produce a documented agreement between inspectors, and that agreement should be the basis of the scoring tolerance in the SLA. Without a documented agreement, the scoring tolerance is a guess, and the SLA loses its defensible anchor.

The documented agreement should also be stored with the inspection records, so that the scoring tolerance in the SLA can be traced back to a decision rather than to an improvisation.

## 2. What are the five essential cleaning KPIs for a janitorial vendor?

A useful cleaning contract does not need ten KPIs. It needs five that cover the decision the client is paying for. Did the work happen. Did it pass inspection. How fast was it redone. And did the frequency hold.

The five essential cleaning KPIs are completion rate, rework rate, inspection score, response time and frequency adherence.

| KPI | What it measures | How it is measured | Typical healthy reference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Completion rate | Share of planned cleaning tasks finished within the scheduled window | Task plan against visit log | 95% to 100% of planned visits completed |
| Rework rate | Share of inspected cleaning points returned for correction | Inspections flagged as defective | Below 8% |
| Inspection score | Quality rating against the scoring rubric | Supervisor or QA inspection across categories | 90/100 or above |
| Response time | Time from defect report to re-clean start | Clock from report to re-clean dispatch | Same shift for guest-facing misses, 24 hours for facility misses |
| Frequency adherence | Share of contracted cleaning days actually executed | Visit log against calendar | 100% within contracted days |

Completion rate answers whether the cleaning happened within the scheduled window. Rework rate answers how much of it had to be redone. Inspection score answers how well it was done. Response time answers how the vendor handled a miss. Frequency adherence answers whether the service was delivered on the contracted cadence.

A contract that tracks only one of the five is a contract with a partial view of the service. The partial views are usually the ones that look fine while the commercial damage accumulates.

For a janitorial vendor selling to a facility manager, the five-KPI set is also the basis of the commercial conversation. The same scope that produces a strong completion rate will produce a weak inspection score when the criteria are loose. The same crew that delivers a strong inspection score will miss response windows when the miss-report channel is informal. The five-KPI set also gives the facility manager a way to compare proposals without flattening every scope into a single hourly number. A proposal that covers completion, inspection and response in one commercial model is auditable. A proposal that hides those terms inside a blended rate is not comparable. The clause architecture that turns these five numbers into contractual teeth is the subject of the [commercial cleaning RFP template](/janitorial-contracts-daily-office-cleaning), and it is where most cleaning agreements either earn their keep or drift into goodwill language.

Each of the five KPIs should have an owner on the facility side and an owner on the vendor side before the first contract is signed. Without named owners, the KPI exists on paper and not in operation, and the weekly dashboard will show the gap rather than the cause.

The delivery block and the quality block should be reported separately in the weekly scorecard, because a facility that wants speed and a facility that wants quality are making different promises. The SLA can reward one without betraying the other, but only if the scorecard shows both.

The same logic applies at the contract level, where the SLA should separate the delivery obligation from the quality obligation into distinct clauses, because a single blended clause produces blended enforcement and blended enforcement produces disputes about which obligation failed.

The delivery obligation and the quality obligation should also have separate owners in the corrective action process, because the team that cleans is not always the team that inspects, and the SLA should make that handoff explicit rather than leaving it to informal practice.

## 3. How do you build a cleaning inspection methodology that produces comparable scores?

An inspection methodology is the measurement system behind the scorecard. Without it, scores are opinions. With it, scores are repeatable.

The methodology runs three tiers of inspection.

| Inspection tier | Who performs it | Coverage | What it catches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-check | Room attendant | 100% of rooms, quick pass | Missed amenities, visible trash, bed presentation |
| Supervisor spot check | Floor supervisor | 15% to 25% of rooms, before release | Bathroom detail, dust lines, linen presentation |
| Manager audit | Executive housekeeper | 3% to 5% of rooms, weekly rotation | Systemic misses, training gaps, standard drift |

The released-room rule applies in commercial cleaning the same way it applies in hospitality. A cleaning task is not complete when the cleaner finishes. It is complete when it passes its assigned check and is released to the facility.

Comparability depends on the criteria list. Each category should be observable, binary where possible, and tied to a consequence. Categories that cannot be observed should not be scored, because a score built on an unmeasurable criterion cannot be defended in an SLA dispute.

In practice, the most defensible cleaning rubrics separate the categories that a guest can see, the categories that a high-touch protocol requires, and the categories that a health inspection or a brand audit samples. Keeping the categories in one written list, with one scoring rule each, is what makes the inspection layer comparable across shifts, across supervisors and across weeks.

The tier structure matters because coverage has to match consequence. A guest-facing cleaning miss has a different operational response than a back-of-house miss, and the tiers should reflect that difference without overlap that makes the scorecard ambiguous.

The tiers should also carry different scoring weights, because a guest-facing miss is usually more costly than a back-office miss of the same severity. The scorecard should therefore apply the weights explicitly rather than relying on a single severity scale.

## 4. What is a good cleaning rework rate, and how is it measured?

The rework rate is the percentage of inspected cleaning points that return for correction.

A rework rate below 8 percent is used as a working threshold in commercial operations. Above that level, the apparent cleaning performance is being propped up by hours that never appear in the productive calculation, and the real cost per cleaning point goes up.

Rework is not only a quality problem. It is a time problem. Every returned inspection consumes supervision time, cleaner time and client trust before the defect is fixed.

The rate is measured from the inspection layer. Each inspection either passes or returns. The returned share is the rework rate. The number should sit on the weekly dashboard next to completion and inspection score, because a high completion rate with a high rework rate is not efficiency. It is speed purchased with quality.

The rework rate should be reported alongside the inspection score, because the two answer different questions. The score says how well the cleaning met the criteria. The rework rate says how much of that cleaning had to be redone. A team can score well and still rework often when criteria are aspirational. A team can rework heavily and still score well when the criteria are lenient. The SLA should therefore reference both, because a contract that optimizes one while ignoring the other will miss the failure mode it was written to prevent.

In commercial buildings, restrooms, lobbies and high-touch points usually carry the highest inspection weight, because they are the areas that produce the most complaints per unit of cleaning. The rework rate should therefore be analyzed by category, not only as an aggregate, so the failure mode is visible before it becomes a pattern.

The threshold should be reviewed whenever the inspection rubric changes, because a looser rubric produces looser scores and a looser score produces a higher rework rate that is really a scoring effect rather than a cleaning effect.

The review should be scheduled, not reactive, because cleaning rubrics evolve with the product, the guest profile and the regulatory environment, and each evolution should be reviewed against the rework data before the new rubric goes live.

## 5. How should the weekly cleaning dashboard report the cleaning KPIs?

The weekly dashboard is the operating rhythm of the cleaning program. It converts the cleaning SLA from a document into a cadence.

| Weekly reporting item | What it captures | Data source | Decision triggered on miss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cleaning scorecard | Completion, rework, inspection score, response time and frequency adherence in one view | Inspection reports plus visit logs | Open corrective action |
| Miss log | Defects found, re-clean window, closure verification | Single miss-report channel | Re-clean within SLA window |
| Trend line | Week-over-week movement on each KPI | Scorecard history | Retraining or renegotiation trigger |

The scorecard is the core artifact. It holds the five KPIs in one place so the facility manager and the vendor review the same numbers. The miss log holds the exceptions, because exceptions are where the average hides the worst days. The trend line holds the history, because a single week proves nothing and a quarter of weeks proves everything.

Without a weekly report, a cleaning program has no memory. The weekly report is what turns a cleaning vendor from a cleaner into a measurable partner.

The dashboard should be reviewed in a standing weekly meeting, with the scorecard presented first and the miss log second, so the discussion starts from the data rather than from the anecdotes that usually come first in cleaning conversations.

The weekly meeting should be short, standing and data-led. The scorecard is reviewed first, the miss log second, and the trend line third, and the decisions that come out of the meeting should be recorded in the corrective action tracker within the same week.

The cadence should also include a monthly review of the scorecard methodology itself, because the scoring rules should evolve as the cleaning program matures, and the SLA targets should be revisited when the methodology changes.

The dashboard should be published in a format that both the facility manager and the vendor can read, because the weekly review requires a shared artifact rather than two separate interpretations of the same cleaning work.

The shared artifact should be the scorecard itself, distributed in a format that both parties can access, because a dashboard that exists only in one tool creates two sources of truth.

## 6. How do you set cleaning SLA targets from a measured baseline?

The SLA target should be derived from a measurement, not from a preference or a promise.

The sequence starts with four steps. Define the criteria list. Measure over a baseline window of four to six weeks. Set the target with a tolerance band. Publish the SLA with the same numbers used for measurement.

A facility manager who sets a cleaning target before measuring is writing an SLA on a guess. The vendor who is asked to hit an unmeasured target is being set up to fail, and the failure will be reported as the vendor's failure even when the crew was never given a fair chance to succeed.

The baseline should capture the actual mix of cleaning tasks, the scheduled windows and the inspection results. From that baseline, the SLA target can be set inside a tolerance band that protects against normal variance while still being specific enough to enforce.

A measured baseline and a priced scope are the two halves of a defensible cleaning agreement.

The tolerance band is what keeps the SLA enforceable without turning ordinary variance into a contractual dispute. A tolerance band of a few percentage points around the measured baseline is typical, and it should be written into the SLA rather than negotiated later under pressure.

The measurement window should be documented in the SLA, because the baseline is only valid for the conditions that produced it. A cleaning program in a high-turnover building will not produce the same baseline as one in a low-turnover building, and the SLA should say which conditions the baseline reflects.

The baseline should be published alongside the SLA, with the measurement conditions, the task mix and the inspection method, so that anyone reading the contract can reproduce the number. A baseline that is not reproducible is a baseline that will be challenged.

The baseline measurement should include the cleaning tasks that are scheduled but not executed, because that is where frequency adherence lives. A facility that measures only execution will miss the compliance problem and only discover it through the miss log.

## 7. What are the contractual consequences when a cleaning KPI is missed?

The consequences are the commercial teeth of the SLA. Without them, the KPIs are suggestions.

| SLA clause | What it specifies | Miss consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Re-clean window | Time from defect report to re-clean completion | Credit per formula or re-clean within window |
| Rework allowance | Number of re-cleans included per period | Additional re-cleans billed at overtime or standard rate |
| Corrective action | Owner, action and closure date for each defect | Escalation to formal plan after repeated misses |

The re-clean window should be written in the same units the KPI uses. If the inspection score is the metric, the consequence should reference the inspection that produced the miss. If the response time is the metric, the consequence should reference the clock from report to re-clean.

The rework allowance should be a number, not a judgment. A facility manager cannot approve a subjective amount of remediation, and a vendor cannot negotiate a subjective number in good faith.

The corrective action clause should name an owner, an action and a closure date for each defect, and should escalate to a formal plan after repeated misses.

Penalties should be proportional to the KPI missed, and the proportionality should be written before the first missed KPI occurs, because retroactive penalty language is usually read as a threat rather than as a term.

The consequence structure should also include a cure period before any penalty takes effect, because an immediate penalty on a first miss is usually disproportionate and becomes a negotiation rather than a control. The cure period should be short, measured in days, and tied to the re-clean window.

## 8. How should a missed cleaning SLA be handled between the facility manager and the vendor?

The handling of a miss should run through a single miss-report channel, such as phone or text, with a defined triage rule.

Misses reported by 9:00 AM should be re-cleaned within 24 hours, and restroom misses should be re-cleaned the same night. Urgent issues during contracted hours should be answered within the response window written in the SLA.

If the vendor misses twice on the same task within 30 days, the standard operating response is to add a supervised visit at no charge and revise the cleaning checklist. That SLA is written into the scope, not promised verbally.

The conversation between the facility manager and the vendor should not be the first place a miss is discussed. The inspection layer, the miss log and the corrective action record should be the first place. The conversation then becomes a review of the records, not a dispute about memory.

The same discipline applies when the miss is systemic rather than isolated. The corrective action record should capture the root cause, the action and the closure date, and the weekly scorecard should show whether the corrective action reduced the rework rate in the following period.

The re-clean should be scheduled against the same criteria that produced the miss, so the corrective action is comparable to the original inspection rather than a generic wipe-down.

The closure record should capture the re-clean date, the verifying inspector and the result, and the miss should be closed only when all three are recorded. A miss that is closed without a verification is a miss that will repeat.

## 9. How do response-time KPIs and cleaning frequency become SLA clauses?

Response time and frequency adherence are the two KPIs that connect the cleaning operation to the service level conversation.

Response time becomes an SLA clause through the miss-report process. The clause should state what is reported, within what window, and what the re-clean expectation is. The KPI measures the clock from report to re-clean start.

Cleaning frequency becomes an SLA clause through the visit log. The contract should state the frequency, the task scope for each frequency, and the consequence of deviation. Frequency adherence is then measured as the share of contracted days executed, which keeps the SLA enforceable without subjective judgment.

Response-time KPIs and frequency adherence together form the operational half of the SLA. The first governs how the vendor handles a miss. The second governs whether the service was delivered at the contracted cadence. A cleaning program that controls response but ignores frequency is managing exceptions while the baseline drifts.

The response-time KPI should be reported as a percentage of contracted hours as well as an absolute number, because the same absolute response can mean very different things depending on the contracted service window and the criticality of the area.

The SLA should therefore treat response time and frequency adherence as linked but distinct obligations, because a vendor can respond quickly and still not be present on the contracted days, and the two failures look identical in a complaint but different in the scorecard.

The reporting should therefore decompose the response KPI into urgency tiers, such as guest-facing, facility and safety-related, because a single response metric can hide the difference between a lobby miss and a restroom miss with a slip hazard.

The urgency tiers should map to the task families in the scope, so that the response expectation is written where the task is defined rather than in a separate policy document that no one reads during an execution.

The response expectations should be tied to the task families in the cleaning scope, so that the SLA expresses the response duty at the point of task definition rather than in a standalone policy that execution teams do not see.

## 10. How do you audit cleaning frequency compliance without subjective judgment?

Frequency compliance is audited through records, not through impressions. The visit log is compared against the cleaning schedule, and the comparison produces the adherence number.

A spot audit can support the review. Sample the building, verify the executed tasks against the schedule, and check that the cleaning that did not run was either absent from the scope or documented as an exception.

Subjective judgment enters only at the inspection layer, where criteria are written and scores are assigned. Once the inspection layer is documented, frequency compliance can be audited as arithmetic, which is the only way it becomes an SLA term.

The audit should be run on a fixed schedule and on an unannounced basis where the contract allows it, because scheduled audits produce theater and unannounced audits produce evidence.

## 11. What is the difference between a cleaning scorecard and a cleaning SLA?

The scorecard is the measurement tool. It defines the criteria, the categories, the scoring and the rework logic.

The service level agreement is the commercial instrument built from the scorecard. It sets the target, the tolerance, the windows and the consequences, and it converts the scorecard into a contract that both parties can enforce.

The two are not competitors. The scorecard produces the number. The SLA turns the number into a term. A scorecard without an SLA is a dashboard. An SLA without a scorecard is a promise.

The scorecard should be maintained by named owners on both sides, and the maintenance cadence should be part of the weekly rhythm rather than an occasional project. That maintenance cadence is what keeps the inspection layer and the scorecard from drifting apart.

The scorecard and the SLA should be versioned together, because a cleaning program that changes its criteria should change its SLA in the same review cycle rather than leaving the two out of sync.

The two documents should be reviewed together on the same cadence, and the review should produce a single set of decisions about criteria, targets and consequences, because separate reviews produce separate sets of assumptions.

The review should produce an action register that feeds the corrective action tracker, so that the decisions about criteria and targets are executed rather than archived in a meeting note.

The action register should be reviewed on the same cadence as the corrective action tracker, and the two should be reconciled at the end of each week, because a decision without an owner and a date is only a wish.

## 12. How do you report cleaning results to management when the vendor owns the process?

The vendor owns the execution. The client owns the outcome, and the outcome has to be reported back to whoever holds the budget.

The weekly reporting package should include the cleaning scorecard, the miss log and the trend line. The facility manager receives the data, reviews it, and owns the escalation path when a target is missed.

Management reporting should separate the operational rhythm from the strategic signal. The operational rhythm is the weekly scorecard. The strategic signal is the trend across weeks, which should trigger a decision about retraining, re-scoping or renegotiation.

The weekly report should be delivered within a defined cadence, and the delivery should include the raw findings, not only the summarized score, so that management can trace a score back to an inspection.

The weekly report should include a summary line for each of the five KPIs, a miss count, an open corrective action count and a trend flag, so that the management review can be completed in the meeting rather than moved to a second meeting. ## 13. How do you renegotiate a cleaning contract after repeated SLA misses?

Repeated misses should move the file from operational response to commercial renegotiation.

The decision sequence runs in order. Check whether the criteria list is still accurate. Check whether the measurement is still valid. Check whether the rework capacity is consuming the productive pool. Check whether the physical flow is the bottleneck. Then consider staffing, re-scoping or renegotiation.

The renegotiation should be data-led. The cleaning scorecard, the miss log and the trend line provide the basis for the conversation, and the facility manager should bring the numbers rather than the frustration.

For a cleaning program that has never been measured, the starting point is the same for most facilities. Build the inspection methodology, run the measurement, set the first SLA targets from the baseline, and report weekly from that point forward.

The renegotiation should also address the scorecard itself, not only the contract language, because a cleaning program that scores differently after retraining should show that in the trend line before any commercial terms are reopened.

The commercial terms should be renegotiated only after the measurement conditions are fixed, because changing the target without changing the conditions would make the new target unmeasurable and the renegotiation pointless.

**What are the most important cleaning contract KPIs?**
The five essential cleaning KPIs are completion rate, rework rate, inspection score, response time and frequency adherence. They replace subjective judgment with measured performance.

**How do you set cleaning SLA targets?**

**What is a good cleaning rework rate?**

**How do you measure cleaning quality without subjective inspections?**
Through a written inspection methodology with observable criteria, scored across categories, with a released-room rule and a supervisor spot check.

**What should be included in a cleaning service level agreement?**
The standard, the evidence list, the reporting cadence, the corrective-action window, the access and screening terms, audit rights and consequences.

**How are cleaning KPIs tied to penalties or credits?**
Through explicit SLA clauses, including the re-clean window, the rework allowance and the corrective action terms, with defined consequences for misses. The penalty should reference the KPI and the tolerance band, and the cure period should precede the penalty.

**How do you audit cleaning frequency compliance?**
Through visit logs against the schedule and spot audits, not through subjective judgment of outcomes. The audit should produce a written finding for each missed task, and the finding should feed the corrective action process rather than sitting as an open observation.

Work the decision sequence in order, then move from operational response to re-scoping or renegotiation with the data in hand. The decision sequence should be documented before the first miss, so that the response to each miss follows the same order rather than being improvised case by case.
