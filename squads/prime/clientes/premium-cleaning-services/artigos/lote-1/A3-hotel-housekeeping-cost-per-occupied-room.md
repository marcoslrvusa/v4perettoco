> **Direct answer:** Cost per occupied room (CPOR) is total housekeeping cost divided by occupied room-nights. In the US market, typical all-in ranges are $9 to $13 in economy hotels, $14 to $19 in select service, $21 to $28 in upscale and $30 to $42 in luxury, once labor, supervision, supplies and linen are counted the same way.

## Key takeaways

- CPOR is the only housekeeping price that survives a change in occupancy: a per-shift rate is a capacity price, a per-room rate is a consumption price, and only the second one can be audited against the PMS.
- Two vendors quoting $1,470 and $1,380 per shift can land 14% apart in the wrong direction once you convert both to cost per delivered credit.
- The invoice hides cost in three predictable places: non-productive hours billed as productive, a room-credit mix that is never reported, and consumables marked up on a base nobody verifies.
- A monthly audit needs four inputs — occupied room-nights, credits delivered, paid hours and loaded rate — and a tolerance band of plus or minus 5% on CPOR after mix adjustment.
- Renegotiation is the last step, not the first: roughly two-thirds of CPOR surprises trace back to the property's own credit table, occupancy data or scope definition.

## 1. What does cost per occupied room actually measure?

Cost per occupied room is a unit cost, and like every unit cost it is only as good as its denominator. The numerator is the total cost of cleaning a room for a given period. The denominator is the number of occupied room-nights in that same period. Divide one by the other and you have the number a controller can put next to ADR, RevPAR and the rooms expense line without translating anything.

The choice of denominator carries more weight than most buyers realize, because three plausible denominators produce three different numbers from the same invoice. Cost per **available** room divides by inventory and moves every time occupancy moves, so it punishes a hotel for a soft month it did not cause. Cost per **sold** room is the accounting term and usually equals occupied rooms, except when it does not: complimentary rooms, house use, employee stays and day-use rooms can be counted as sold in some property management systems and excluded in others. Cost per **occupied** room-night is the operational denominator, and it is the one that matches labor consumption, linen consumption and amenity consumption.

The definition also needs an explicit boundary for out-of-order rooms. An out-of-order room is not occupied and does not consume a full clean, but it may still consume a check, a dust sheet or a light touch. Properties that leave OOO rooms in the denominator inflate CPOR by 1% to 3% in normal months and by much more during renovation. The audit rule is simple: denominator equals occupied room-nights as reported by the PMS at the same cut-off time used for the revenue report.

What CPOR does not tell you is equally important. It does not measure quality, it does not measure the mix of checkouts and stayovers, and it does not tell you whether the standard behind the price is achievable. Those three questions sit outside the number and have to be audited separately, which is why an invoice review that stops at CPOR is a price check rather than an audit.

Used properly, CPOR answers one question with uncomfortable precision: what did it cost, per occupied room, to deliver the cleaning standard this property actually received last month? Every other number in the conversation is a proxy for that one.

## 2. Why is CPOR more useful than a price per shift or per square foot?

Most housekeeping contracts in the US market are still quoted per shift, per attendant or per square foot. Each of those formats is defensible at the moment of signature and nearly useless twelve months later, because none of them is tied to the thing the hotel actually buys: a clean, released, occupied room.

A per-shift price is a capacity price. It tells you what eight attendants cost for eight hours. It says nothing about how many credits those eight attendants deliver. Two crews at the same shift price can differ by 25% in output, and the invoice will look identical. The buyer has purchased availability and is now hoping that availability converts into rooms.

CPOR converts both formats into a common unit. The arithmetic is unforgiving and that is the point. Take an invoice of $1,470 per shift for eight attendants who deliver 196 credits, and an invoice of $1,380 per shift for eight attendants who deliver 158 credits. The naive reading is that the second vendor is 6% cheaper. Convert both:

- Vendor A: $1,470 ÷ 196 credits = $7.50 per credit.
- Vendor B: $1,380 ÷ 158 credits = $8.73 per credit.

Vendor A is 14% cheaper per delivered credit while charging 6.5% more per shift. Nothing in the shift price revealed that; only the conversion did. When the same comparison is repeated at the property's real credit mix, the gap either holds or collapses, and either outcome is actionable. This is the same logic that separates a real proposal from a decorated one in the [commercial cleaning cost guide for New Jersey](/insights/commercial-cleaning-cost-nj-pricing-guide-2026), where the per-shift and per-room formats are reconciled line by line.

There is a second reason CPOR earns its place: it is the only cleaning metric that can be plotted against RevPAR. When CPOR grows faster than RevPAR for three consecutive months, either the service standard changed or the invoice did. That single chart replaces an hour of argument in a monthly owner review, and it is the reason the number belongs in the operating statement rather than in a vendor file.

## 3. What belongs in CPOR, and what has to stay outside it?

Before any comparison, two parties have to agree on the fence. The most common source of a "cheaper" proposal is not a lower price; it is a narrower definition of what the price covers. The matrix below is the one that holds up in an audit, because every line has a reason attached to it rather than a preference.

| Cost item | In CPOR? | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Room attendant wages and payroll burden | Yes | The primary driver of room labor |
| Floor supervision | Yes, allocated by hours | Rooms are not released without it |
| Management and administrative fee | Yes, when invoiced | Part of the delivered service, not an extra |
| Consumables used inside the room | Yes | Variable with occupancy, therefore part of the unit cost |
| Linen and terry processing | Yes | Consumed per occupied room-night |
| Uniforms, recruiting, training | Yes, allocated | Recurring cost of holding a crew |
| Overtime premium | Yes, but flagged separately | Signal of a standard or scheduling problem |
| Agency and temporary labor | Yes, flagged separately | The price of vacancies, not of cleaning |
| Public area cleaning inside scope | Yes, allocated per occupied room | Contracted scope with its own labor |
| Deep cleans and project work | No | Not occupancy-driven; belongs to a separate schedule |
| Guest laundry, valet, minibar restock | No, unless contracted | Different service line with different economics |
| Sales tax and pass-through fees | No | Neither cost nor margin |
| Cancellation and no-service penalties | No | Commercial term, not a cleaning cost |

Two rules make the matrix work in practice.

**The fence is written before the bid, not after.** A property that circulates an inclusion sheet with the request for proposal receives comparable bids. A property that circulates a room count receives eight different products at eight different prices, and the cheapest one will usually be the narrowest.

**The fence does not move between months.** If consumables were inside the unit cost in January, they cannot migrate to a separate invoice in June without a formal change order. Scope migration is the quietest form of price increase in the category, and it never appears as a rate change.

There is one legitimate exception. Deep cleans, carpet extraction, grout restoration and post-renovation work are genuinely project costs. They should be removed from CPOR and tracked against a separate annual calendar, because a month with a floor-wide carpet extraction will otherwise look like a vendor problem when it is a scheduled event. The [hotel deep clean calendar](/hotel-cleaning-services-new-jersey) logic applies here: planned work gets planned, unplanned work gets argued about.

## 4. How do you calculate CPOR step by step?

The calculation has six steps, and the only one that usually goes wrong is the second. Work through them in order and the result is reproducible by a controller, a general manager and a vendor independently.

**Step 1 — Pull occupied room-nights from the PMS.** Thirty consecutive days, same cut-off as the revenue report, out-of-order rooms excluded. Record the figure; it is the denominator for everything that follows.

**Step 2 — Convert room status into credits.** This is where most properties improvise. Use a published credit table: a checkout at 1.00, a light stayover at 0.50, a full stayover at 0.70, a turndown at 0.20, public areas at a fixed allocation per occupied room. Multiply each status count by its credit value and sum.

**Step 3 — Establish productive hours.** Take paid hours from the invoice, then subtract meal periods, breaks, pre-shift lineup, cart setup, linen trips, training and end-of-shift administration. If the vendor cannot provide the split, ask for the shift structure and apply a measured share rather than a guess. A defensible range in most US operations is 82% to 88% productive.

**Step 4 — Divide credits by productive hours.** The result is the delivered standard. Compare it with the contracted standard. A gap larger than 0.2 credits per hour is a finding, not a rounding difference.

**Step 5 — Add the non-labor lines.** Consumables, linen processing, supervision, administrative fee and any allocated overhead. Each line is divided by occupied room-nights and added to labor cost per room.

**Step 6 — Reconcile the total against the invoice.** The sum of your line-level CPOR should equal the invoice total divided by occupied room-nights, within rounding. If it does not, a line is missing or double counted, and the missing line is usually supervision or consumables.

A worked example makes the arithmetic concrete. A 140-room select-service property at 78% occupancy for 30 days produces 3,276 occupied room-nights. Its credit mix converts to 0.93 credits per occupied room-night, so total credits are 3,047. At a contracted standard of 3.2 credits per productive hour, that requires 952 productive hours. At an 85% productive share, paid hours are 1,120. At a loaded rate of $28.50, labor is $31,920, or $9.74 per occupied room. Supervision at 140 hours and $38.50 adds $5,390, or $1.65. Consumables at $1.85 and linen at $3.10 add $4.95. Administrative overhead at 8% of labor plus supervision adds $2,985, or $0.91.

Total all-in cost is $56,512. CPOR is $17.25. That number sits inside the select-service band and, more importantly, it can now be rebuilt from four inputs in ten minutes. The [housekeeping cost and pricing guide](/hotel-housekeeping-cost-pricing-guide-2026) carries the same decomposition at proposal level, which is where the comparison actually has to happen.

## 5. What are typical CPOR ranges in the US market?

The ranges below describe conventional cart-based operations with an inspection layer, in markets where the loaded attendant rate sits between $26 and $34 per hour. They are operating ranges, not standards, and they move with wage market, union status, room size and service model.

| Property tier | Productive minutes per occupied room | Labor cost per occupied room | Supplies and linen per occupied room | All-in CPOR range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Economy / limited service | 12 – 16 | $6.70 – $8.90 | $2.60 – $3.40 | $9 – $13 |
| Select service | 16 – 21 | $8.90 – $11.70 | $3.60 – $4.90 | $14 – $19 |
| Full service, upscale | 26 – 34 | $14.50 – $19.00 | $5.20 – $7.30 | $21 – $28 |
| Luxury | 38 – 50 | $21.20 – $27.90 | $7.40 – $10.60 | $30 – $42 |
| Resort / large suites | 50 – 70 | $27.90 – $39.10 | $9.80 – $15.20 | $40 – $58 |

Four cautions before anyone benchmarks against this table.

**Market wage is the largest single variable.** A select-service property in a suburban market with a $24 loaded rate and a Manhattan property with a $38 loaded rate are both operating correctly at CPOR figures 40% apart. Comparing across markets without normalizing the loaded rate produces conclusions that are really about labor geography.

**Minutes per occupied room is the honest column.** It is the one number that survives a wage difference, because it measures work rather than money. A property that does not know its productive minutes per occupied room cannot tell whether a high CPOR is a wage problem or a work problem.

**The mix moves the number inside the range.** A property running 55% checkouts sits at the top of its tier; a property running 65% stayovers sits at the bottom. Neither is overpaying.

**Luxury and resort figures include presentation labor that other tiers do not.** Turndown, linen changes on request, amenity programs and multiple daily touches are labor, and they belong in the range rather than in a separate document that nobody reads.

## 6. Where does the invoice hide non-productive hours?

Non-productive hours are the single largest source of unexplained cost in a housekeeping invoice, and they are hidden by a definition rather than by an error. The invoice bills paid hours. The room consumes productive hours. The gap between the two is real work that the property genuinely needs — lineup, breaks, cart setup, linen runs — but it is not room work, and it should be priced and disclosed as overhead rather than absorbed into a per-room rate that pretends otherwise.

The gap is larger than most buyers assume. Common US operating structures produce a productive share between 82% and 88% on a straight eight-hour shift with one meal period and two breaks. Properties with multiple towers, distant linen rooms or a paid pre-shift lineup fall to 78% or lower. A vendor that bills 100% of paid hours as productive and prices the per-room rate accordingly is not lying — it is distributing the same total cost across fewer apparent hours, which makes the rate look higher and the standard look lower than both really are.

The arithmetic of the distortion is straightforward. A loaded rate of $28.50 at an 85% productive share is an effective $33.53 per productive hour. At a 78% share, the same $28.50 becomes $36.54. That is a 9% difference in the true cost of an hour of room work, produced entirely by definition. Two vendors quoting the same $28.50 can therefore be 9% apart, and neither has changed a wage.

Three tests expose the gap quickly.

1. **Cross-check hours against rooms.** Divide paid hours by occupied room-nights. A select-service property typically lands between 0.29 and 0.35 paid hours per occupied room. A figure above 0.38 with a normal credit mix means either the productive share is low or the crew is carrying work that is not in the credit table.
2. **Ask for the shift structure in writing.** Start time, lineup duration, break schedule, meal period, cart setup allowance and end-of-shift close-out. A vendor that answers precisely is managing the gap; one that answers vaguely is passing it to you.
3. **Compare the same standard at two productive shares.** Rebuild the invoice at 85% and at 78% and see how much of the price difference between two bids is definition rather than delivery.

## 7. What should a housekeeping invoice actually show?

An invoice that cannot be audited is not a neutral document; it is a document that shifts risk to the payer. The format below is the minimum that allows a monthly reconciliation without a formal dispute, and it is close to what most serious commercial programs already produce for their hotel accounts.

| Invoice element | What it must show | What you verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Room-nights | Occupied rooms by day, from PMS | Matches revenue report at cut-off |
| Credits delivered | Count by status: checkout, stayover, turndown, suite | Reconciles to your credit table |
| Paid hours | By day, by shift, by classification | Plausible against rooms and standard |
| Productive hours | Paid hours net of breaks, lineup, setup | Share between 82% and 88% |
| Loaded rate | Base wage, burden, and what burden covers | Burden between 26% and 38% |
| Supervision | Hours and rate, allocated basis stated | Ratio of 12 to 18 attendants per supervisor |
| Consumables | Units or per-room allowance, with markup stated | Markup disclosed, not embedded |
| Linen | Pounds or pieces processed, or per-room rate | Volume tracks occupancy |
| Rework | Rooms returned and re-cleaned, if billed | Should be zero or capped |
| Escalation | Index, base month, cap, notice period | Applied to the correct base |
| Minimum call | Conditions that trigger it | Frequency by day of week |

Two structural details matter more than the rest.

**Granularity by day, not by month.** A monthly total of 1,120 hours is unauditable. The same hours split across 30 days reveal the Tuesday peak, the Saturday minimum-call trigger and the two days when the crew was short. Day-level data is also what makes the difference between a scheduling problem and a standard problem visible, which is the distinction that decides whether the fix is operational or commercial.

**Credit count separate from hour count.** Hours tell you what was paid for. Credits tell you what was delivered. An invoice that reports only hours has removed the numerator from the productivity ratio and left the buyer with a cost and no output. Any [hotel turnover cleaning checklist](/hotel-turnover-cleaning-checklist) used operationally should feed the same status counts that appear on the invoice, so the two documents cannot disagree.

## 8. How does the checkout-to-stayover mix distort CPOR?

CPOR is stable in a way that the work behind it is not. A checkout consumes roughly twice the labor of a light stayover, so a month with a heavier departure pattern genuinely costs more per occupied room, and an invoice that does not move when the mix moves is more suspicious than one that does.

The elasticity is easy to estimate and worth putting on one line of the audit. In the worked example, a blended credit value of 0.93 per occupied room-night assumed roughly 40% checkouts and 55% stayovers, with the remainder in turndown and suites. If the checkout share rises ten points to 50%, the blended credit value rises to about 0.98 — a 5.4% increase in credits for the same occupied room count. Because labor is roughly 66% of all-in CPOR in that tier, the expected CPOR increase is about 3.0%, or $0.52 per room.

That single calculation changes the entire tone of a monthly review. A vendor invoice that rises 3% in a month with a ten-point mix shift is performing exactly as designed. The same invoice rising 3% in a month with an unchanged mix is a 3% price increase wearing a costume. Without a mix report, the property cannot tell the two apart and will usually argue about the wrong one.

Five mix events are common enough to be named in the contract.

- **Early check-in and late checkout.** Both create rooms that are cleaned twice or cleaned under time pressure, and the labor is real even when the room-night count does not change.
- **Day-use rooms.** Occupied for hours, cleaned like a checkout, counted as one room-night. In airport and urban properties these can reach 4% to 7% of inventory and distort CPOR downward.
- **Complimentary and house-use rooms.** Cleaned, not sold. If they are excluded from the denominator, CPOR rises for reasons that have nothing to do with the vendor.
- **Extended-stay and apartment-style inventory.** Kitchens, dishes and longer stays shift the credit table substantially, and a property that applies a standard room credit to a suite with a kitchenette will underpay the work and overpay the audit.

The audit rule that follows is simple: no mix report, no variance discussion. A vendor that cannot produce a status count by day is asking to be judged on a single number that its own operations determine. A property that accepts that arrangement has outsourced not just the cleaning but the measurement.

## 9. Which allocation errors make two proposals incomparable?

Most bid comparisons fail before the price is examined, because the two documents allocate cost differently and the difference is invisible at the total. Nine allocation errors account for nearly all of the confusion in the category.

**Suites counted as one room.** A two-bathroom suite carries 1.5 to 2.0 credits. A vendor that counts it as one room is quoting a lower rate for a heavier room, and the shortfall appears later as a change order or as skipped detail.

**Public areas allocated by square footage instead of by labor.** Lobby, restrooms, elevators, corridors and outlets are not proportional to area. A property with a large but lightly used ballroom will underpay; one with dense public restrooms will overpay.

**Supervision allocated as a percentage instead of by hours.** A flat 10% supervision load hides the difference between one supervisor for twelve attendants and one for twenty-two. The second structure looks cheaper and inspects less.

**Rework billed as additional hours.** If the contract includes quality, rework is a vendor cost. If rework appears as a billed line, the property is paying twice for the same room and the rework rate becomes a revenue source.

**Consumables markup hidden in the room rate.** Two proposals at the same CPOR can differ by 6% in true cost if one bundles a 35% consumables markup and the other discloses a 15% markup on a larger base.

**Minimum call applied to low-occupancy days.** A four-hour minimum on a Sunday with 40 occupied rooms is a fixed cost that belongs in the proposal, not in a surprise line at month end.

**Escalation applied to a base that includes fees.** A 3% annual increase applied to a base that already contains a management fee and consumables markup compounds two increases into one.

The remedy is a normalization sheet: one page, one column per vendor, every line converted to the same unit. Total cost, occupied rooms, credits, paid hours, effective rate, CPOR. A vendor that refuses to fill the sheet is not necessarily hiding something, but it is declining to be compared, and that is itself information.

## 10. What does a monthly invoice audit look like in practice?

An audit that runs every month takes less time than the argument it prevents, provided the sequence is fixed and the tolerances are agreed in advance. The model below is built for a 30-day cycle and assumes the invoice format in section seven.

| Check | Tolerance | Action if outside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Occupied room-nights vs PMS | Zero difference | Hold payment on the disputed share |
| Credits delivered vs credit table | Plus or minus 2% | Request status detail by day |
| Paid hours vs prior month, mix-adjusted | Plus or minus 3% | Request shift-level breakdown |
| Productive share | 82% to 88% | Recalculate effective rate |
| Credits per productive hour vs contract | Plus or minus 0.2 | Variance meeting within five days |
| Labor cost per occupied room | Plus or minus 4% | Rebuild invoice from inputs |
| Consumables per occupied room | Plus or minus 6% | Request consumption detail |
| Linen per occupied room | Plus or minus 6% | Reconcile against poundage or pieces |
| Supervision share of invoice | 9% to 14% | Review span of control |
| Rework rooms billed | Zero | Dispute the line |
| Overtime share of hours | Below 5% | Diagnose scheduling or standard |
| Escalation applied | Contract index and base | Correct and reissue |

Run the audit on a fixed calendar rather than on the invoice's arrival. Day 1: invoice received with the data package. Day 2 and 3: property reconciles rooms and credits. Day 4: finance rebuilds the cost per line. Day 5: variance review with the vendor. Day 10: dispute window closes. A cycle that depends on someone remembering to start will run three weeks late and lose the leverage that comes from paying on time.

Four rules keep the audit from becoming adversarial.

**Publish the tolerances in the contract.** A tolerance agreed in advance is a quality control. A tolerance invented after the fact is a negotiation, and the party with the invoice usually wins it.

**Review exceptions, not averages.** A month that lands at $17.20 against a $17.25 baseline is not interesting. The three days with 0.29 credits per hour are, because they explain next month.

**Separate the operational finding from the commercial claim.** A rising CPOR with a stable mix is commercial. A rising CPOR with a rising checkout share is operational and expected. Mixing the two in one conversation guarantees that neither gets resolved.

## 11. What does a bad invoice look like?

Experienced facility and rooms directors read invoices the way clinicians read charts: the abnormality is usually in the pattern rather than in any single number. Eight patterns recur across the category.

**Round numbers in every month.** A credit count of exactly 3,000 in February and exactly 3,000 in March is not a coincidence; it is an estimate. Estimated credits cannot be audited and should not be paid as if they were measured.

**Hours that do not move with occupancy.** A property that swings from 62% to 88% occupancy and reports the same 1,120 hours in both months is either overstaffed in the soft month or under-cleaned in the peak. Both are expensive, and only one is visible.

**No status breakdown.** An invoice that reports rooms cleaned without distinguishing checkouts, stayovers and suites has removed the only variable that explains legitimate variance.

**Supervision above 14% of invoice value.** Some properties genuinely need dense supervision. Most do not, and a supervision line that grows faster than the crew is a span-of-control problem priced as a service improvement.

**Consumables above 9% of total invoice value.** In most select-service and upscale operations, consumables land between 8% and 12% of all-in cost when linen is separated. A figure that drifts upward without an occupancy explanation is usually a markup change.

**A recurring miscellaneous line.** "Sundries", "supplies" and "additional services" are legitimate categories, but a line without a unit, a quantity and a rate is not a cost; it is a rounding error with a name.

**Rework billed back to the property.** Any invoice that charges for re-cleaning rooms that failed inspection has converted a quality metric into a revenue line.

**Escalation applied to the wrong base or the wrong month.** This is the most common arithmetic error in the category and the easiest to correct once the contract clause is read carefully.

## 12. How do you compare proposals built on different bases?

The comparison has to happen at the property's real occupancy and the property's real credit mix, otherwise it measures the proposals' assumptions rather than their economics. The table below takes the two vendors introduced in section two and audits both.

| Vendor | Shift price | Claimed credits per hour | Claimed cost per credit | Delivered credits per shift (audited) | Audited cost per credit | Labor CPOR at 0.93 credits per room |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $1,470 | 3.6 | $7.50 | 163 | $9.02 | $8.39 |
| B | $1,380 | 2.9 | $8.73 | 158 | $8.73 | $8.12 |

Read the columns in order. On the claim, Vendor A is cheaper per credit by 14%. On the audit, Vendor A delivers 163 credits per shift rather than the 196 implied by its claim, and its cost per credit rises to $9.02 — 3.3% more expensive than B. The shift price did not change. The claim did.

This is why the audit must include a delivered-credit test on historical accounts, not just a reference check. Three questions produce the number: how many credits per shift were actually delivered in the last six months, how many productive hours were in those shifts, and what happened to the rework rate. A vendor with a 3.6 claim and a 3.0 record is not dishonest; it is quoting a standard it can hit on a good Tuesday and cannot hit on a group Monday. The contract should price the standard it can hit in the worst week of the quarter, not the best.

The second dimension of comparison is fixed versus variable structure. A proposal with a high fixed component — minimum call, guaranteed supervision, fixed consumables allowance — is cheaper at high occupancy and more expensive at low occupancy. A proposal with a high variable component is the reverse. Neither is better in the abstract. The right structure depends on the property's occupancy volatility, and the way to decide is to model both proposals at the property's 10th percentile and 90th percentile occupancy rather than at the average.

## 13. Should you renegotiate, or fix your own data first?

The instinct when CPOR rises is to reopen the contract. In practice, roughly two-thirds of the surprises that reach a renegotiation trace back to the property's own data: a credit table that never absorbed a new room type, an occupancy figure pulled at the wrong cut-off, or a scope definition that quietly moved project work into the recurring invoice.

Work the sequence before calling the vendor.

1. **Has the credit table changed?** New suite category, new amenity program, new linen policy, renovation. If yes, the CPOR change is a definition change and the contract is fine.
2. **Is the mix-adjusted comparison correct?** Rebuild CPOR at the prior month's credit mix. Half of apparent increases disappear at this step.
3. **Is the occupancy denominator right?** Check the PMS cut-off, the treatment of comp and house-use rooms, and out-of-order inventory.
4. **Is the scope identical?** Compare the current invoice's line items to the original. A new consumables line or a reclassified supervision line explains more variance than a rate change.
5. **Is the delivered standard intact?** If credits per productive hour fell while CPOR rose, the issue is delivery and the conversation is operational.
6. **Is the market rate actually out of line?** Only now compare the loaded rate to the local market. If the rate is within 5% of market and the standard is intact, there is nothing to renegotiate.

Renegotiation is the right move in four situations, and each has evidence attached.

- **Standard drift without a mix change.** Credits per productive hour down more than 0.3 for three consecutive months, with hours flat.
- **Structural overtime.** Overtime above 8% of hours for two quarters, with no occupancy explanation and no schedule change.
- **Consumables markup out of market.** A disclosed markup more than ten points above comparable proposals, or an undisclosed markup that cannot be reconciled to unit costs.
- **Escalation applied incorrectly.** The clause is explicit; the correction is arithmetic and should be requested in writing.

Renegotiation is the wrong move when the property cannot produce a mix-adjusted baseline. In that situation the conversation is a price argument dressed as an audit, and the party holding the invoice data usually wins it. The [hidden costs of inconsistent cleaning](/insights/hidden-costs-inconsistent-commercial-cleaning-nj-ny) are instructive here: most of the cost that owners try to recover through price pressure is actually created upstream, in scheduling, scope and measurement, and it survives the renegotiation because it was never in the contract.

## 14. How do you turn CPOR into a weekly operating tool?

Monthly CPOR is an audit instrument. Weekly CPOR is a management instrument, and the difference is the lag. A monthly number tells a property in March what went wrong in February. A rolling four-week number tells it on Thursday what changed on Monday.

The weekly view needs three cuts, and no more.

**CPOR by segment.** Transient, group and contract business carry different arrival patterns and different cleaning loads. A property that reports one blended CPOR will miss the group block that arrives at 11 p.m. and departs at 6 a.m., which is the single most expensive pattern in the category.

**CPOR against occupancy, on the same axis.** Two lines on one chart answer the question the invoice cannot: is the cost rising because the hotel is fuller, or because the cost per room is rising? The first is business. The second is a finding.

**CPOR against RevPAR.** When CPOR grows 6% and RevPAR grows 9%, the department is gaining efficiency in relative terms even though the absolute number rose. When the relationship inverts for two consecutive months, the conversation moves to the standard, the schedule or the contract, in that order.

The operating rhythm that makes this work fits in twenty minutes. Monday: rooms and credits for the week closed. Tuesday: hours and rate confirmed with the vendor. Wednesday: CPOR computed at the segment level. Thursday: exceptions reviewed, meaning the two heaviest days, the two lowest credit days and any day with overtime above 8%. Friday: one action, assigned to one person, with a date.

## 15. What must the contract state for the audit to work in month twelve?

Every audit right in the world is worthless if the contract does not define the units, the data and the calendar. The clauses below are the ones that determine whether the reconciliation in month twelve is a ten-minute exercise or a dispute.

**Credit table as an exhibit.** Values by room status and suite type, attached to the agreement, revised only by written change order. Without this, the delivered-credit test has no denominator.

**Definition of productive hours.** Explicit exclusions for meal periods, breaks, lineup, cart setup, linen trips, training and administration, with the expected productive share stated as a range.

**Monthly data package.** Occupied room-nights, credits by status, paid and productive hours by day and shift, consumables units, linen volume, rework rooms and supervision hours. Delivered by day five, in a stated format.

**Audit right with a defined window.** The property may audit or appoint an auditor within 90 days of invoice receipt, with access to the vendor's time and production records for the account.

**Mix-adjustment clause.** CPOR variance is assessed at constant credit mix, and the vendor supplies the mix detail needed to make that adjustment.

**Rework at vendor cost.** Rooms returned for correction are re-cleaned at no charge, and the rework rate is reported monthly against a stated threshold.

**Escalation with a named index, base month and cap.** Applied once a year, to the labor base only, never compounded across fee lines.

**Minimum call defined by day and condition.** Stated as a number of hours and a number of days, not as a discretionary practice.

**Termination for sustained KPI miss.** Two consecutive quarters below the contracted standard or above the CPOR tolerance band, with a cure period.

**Survival of the audit clause.** The right to reconcile survives termination for twelve months, which is the only way the final invoice can be closed honestly.

The commercial logic is not adversarial. A partner that has built its pricing from a credit table and a productive-hour definition wants the same definitions on the other side of the table, because they are the only defense against a client who compares a per-room rate to a per-shift bid and concludes the wrong thing. ## FAQ

**What is a good cost per occupied room?**
For a select-service hotel in a typical US labor market, $14 to $19 all-in is a normal range. Economy properties run $9 to $13, upscale $21 to $28, luxury $30 to $42. The right comparison is against your own prior months at constant mix, not against a national average.

**How do you calculate cost per occupied room?**
Divide total housekeeping cost by occupied room-nights for the same period. Total cost includes labor, supervision, supplies, linen and allocated administrative fee. Occupied room-nights come from the PMS at the same cut-off used for the revenue report, with out-of-order rooms excluded.

**Should CPOR include supplies and linen?**
Yes, if you want a number you can compare with another property or another proposal. Excluding them produces a labor-only rate that looks 30% cheaper and explains nothing. The only items that stay outside are project work, sales tax and pass-through fees.

**Why does CPOR change when occupancy changes?**
Because part of the cost is fixed. Supervision, minimum call and administrative allocation do not fall proportionally when occupancy drops, so CPOR rises in soft months and falls in strong ones. That is why comparisons should be made at constant occupancy as well as constant mix.

**What is the difference between cost per occupied room and cost per available room?**
Cost per available room divides by total inventory, so it moves with occupancy and is useful for budgeting. Cost per occupied room divides by rooms actually sold, so it measures the cost of the work performed. For vendor audits, occupied is the correct denominator.

**How many paid hours should housekeeping bill per occupied room?**
In select service, 0.29 to 0.35 paid hours per occupied room is a common range. Above 0.38 with a normal credit mix, either the productive share is low or work outside the credit table is being absorbed into the invoice.

**What is a normal supervisor ratio on an invoice?**
Twelve to eighteen room attendants per floor supervisor is a practical range, with dedicated coverage for inspection. Supervision usually lands between 9% and 14% of invoice value. Above that, ask what the extra layer is inspecting.

**Can I audit an invoice without a room credit table?**
Not meaningfully. Without credit values by room status, you can measure cost but not output, and a cost without an output cannot be judged. Building the credit table is the first deliverable, not the last.

**How often should housekeeping invoices be reconciled?**
Monthly, on a fixed calendar with a five-day review and a ten-day dispute window. Weekly reconciliation of rooms, credits and hours is worth doing once the monthly process is stable, because it catches schedule problems before they become invoice problems.

**What should trigger a renegotiation?**
Standard drift of more than 0.3 credits per hour for three months with a stable mix, overtime above 8% of hours for two quarters, a consumables markup more than ten points out of market, or an escalation applied to the wrong base. A single high month is not a trigger.
