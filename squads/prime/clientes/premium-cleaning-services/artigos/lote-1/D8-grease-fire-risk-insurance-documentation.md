> **Direct answer:** A grease fire starts when cooking oil is heated past its autoignition point and the vapor meets an ignition source, and it spreads through hood filters and duct grease. Preventing it means cleaning to a documented interval, keeping the exhaust free of buildup, and holding records an insurer and fire inspector can verify.

## Key takeaways

- Grease becomes a fire problem in two stages: the pan fire at the appliance, and the exhaust fire that follows the grease film inside the hood, filters and duct.
- The exhaust system is the part of the kitchen that converts a contained cooking fire into a building fire, because the duct is lined with fuel and pulls air upward.
- Underwriters do not ask whether the kitchen was cleaned; they ask for the record that proves it, which is why the certificate, the photos and the dated log carry as much weight as the service itself.
- A cleaning program that cannot show interval compliance, scope and technician identity is a program that pays premiums without buying insurability.
- Most claim failures are documentation failures: a missed visit that was never recorded, a certificate with no before-and-after photos, or a report that describes the hood but not the duct.

## 1. How does grease actually start a restaurant fire?

A grease fire is not a single event. It is a sequence with three stages, and each stage has a different prevention lever. Understanding the sequence is what separates a cleaning program from a certificate collection.

The first stage is the pan. Cooking oil heated in a fryer, a sauté pan or on a flat top reaches its smoke point, then its flash point, then its fire point. Once the oil is hot enough to sustain combustion, the vapor above the surface can ignite from a burner flame, a hot element, a pilot light or even a spark from equipment. Cooking oils commonly cited in fire investigation reach autoignition somewhere between roughly 600°F and 700°F, which is well inside the range a commercial fryer can reach when the thermostat fails or when oil is added to an already hot vat.

The second stage is the surface. A pan fire that is not extinguished spreads to the grease film that coats the hood, the filter frames, the backsplash and the wall behind the cooking line. That film is thin, which is exactly why it is dangerous: thin layers heat quickly, produce vapor quickly and re-ignite after a suppression discharge if the metal underneath was never cleaned.

The third stage is the exhaust. Once fire enters the hood plenum, the filters and the duct, the system stops being a ventilation path and becomes a fuel path. The duct carries a continuous grease coating, the fan pulls air and flame toward the roof, and the vertical run behaves like a chimney. Firefighters describe duct fires as some of the most difficult restaurant incidents to extinguish because the fire is inside a metal enclosure that is hot, inaccessible and often hidden above a ceiling.

The practical consequence is that prevention has to address all three stages at once. A kitchen that trains staff to smother a pan fire but never cleans the duct has protected the smallest fire and left the largest one unmanaged. Grease also polymerizes with heat, turning into a varnish-like layer that ordinary degreaser will not remove, which is why hood and duct cleaning is a specialized scope rather than a closing task.

## 2. Where does grease accumulate in a commercial kitchen?

Grease accumulates wherever hot vapor condenses, and hot vapor goes where the air goes. That means the accumulation map follows the exhaust path first and the surrounding surfaces second. Most kitchens clean the visible parts of that map and miss the parts that carry the fire risk.

The hood canopy catches the first and heaviest load. Filters intercept a large share of the aerosolized grease, which is why filter cleaning frequency is a separate operational question from duct cleaning frequency. Above and behind the filters sits the plenum, a cavity that is out of sight, hot and coated. The duct carries what the filters did not catch, and the fan housing at the roof concentrates the load at the point of highest mechanical energy.

Horizontal duct runs are worse than vertical runs for accumulation, because grease settles where velocity drops. Elbows, transitions and damper blades collect a film that thickens with every service week. Rooftop fan housings and the curb around them are frequently the last thing anyone inspects and the first thing a fire investigator photographs.

Inside the kitchen, the accumulation points are less obvious. The wall behind the fryer and the range absorbs vapor and turns tacky within weeks. The lip of the hood, the grease cup, the filter frame channels and the caulking line where stainless meets wall all hold film. Floor and wall seams collect grease that migrates downward and becomes a slip and ignition risk at ankle height.

| Accumulation point | Why it collects | Fire exposure | How it is verified |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hood canopy and lip | Condensation of hot vapor on cool stainless | Direct flame contact from the cooking line | Visual and wipe test at inspection |
| Filters and filter frames | Interception of aerosolized grease | Ignition source inches above the appliance | Filter removal and frame inspection |
| Hood plenum | Low-velocity cavity behind filters | Fire enters here first after a pan fire | Access panel inspection with light |
| Horizontal duct runs | Velocity drop at elbows and transitions | Sustained fuel bed above the ceiling | Duct interior inspection at access ports |
| Vertical duct and riser | Chimney effect and film coating | Rapid vertical spread toward the roof | Camera or physical inspection of the run |
| Rooftop fan and housing | Highest mechanical energy, continuous airflow | Fan failure and fire at the roof penetration | Fan housing inspection and grease trap check |
| Wall behind cooking line | Vapor condensation on vertical surface | Re-ignition after suppression discharge | Wipe test at hand height and above |
| Floor and wall seams | Downward migration and pooling | Floor-level fire spread and slip risk | Detail cleaning verification at the seam |

Two conclusions follow from this map. First, the highest-risk accumulation is not the most visible accumulation. The hood front that a manager sees every shift is the least informative surface in the kitchen, because it is cleaned most often. Second, the verification method has to match the location: a wipe test at hand height proves nothing about a duct run above a ceiling.

## 3. Why does the exhaust system carry the highest fire load in the building?

The exhaust system carries the highest fire load because it is the only part of the building designed to move hot, grease-laden air continuously, and because it stores the byproduct of that movement on its own surfaces. A kitchen can be spotless at floor level and still have a duct full of fuel above the ceiling.

Three properties make the exhaust uniquely hazardous. The first is continuity. The duct runs from the cooking surface to the roof, which means a fire that enters the system has an uninterrupted path to the building envelope. The second is confinement. The fire burns inside a metal enclosure, which limits access for extinguishment and concentrates heat on the structure around it. The third is draft. The fan keeps moving air, which feeds oxygen to the fire and pushes it toward the point of least resistance, usually the roof.

The chimney effect deserves separate attention. A vertical duct with a hot fire at the bottom and an open termination at the top creates a pressure difference that accelerates the fire upward. The same principle that makes a fireplace draw makes a grease-coated riser dangerous. This is why duct fires are frequently reported as roof fires by the time the fire department arrives, even though the ignition was at the cooking line.

Access determines whether the risk can be managed at all. A duct without access panels cannot be inspected, and a duct that cannot be inspected cannot be cleaned to a verifiable standard. Many older kitchens in dense urban markets have exhaust runs that were built without adequate access, and the cleaning scope quietly becomes "what can be reached" rather than "what exists". An insurer reading a certificate that covers the hood but not the duct is reading a document about a different system.

The filter is the hinge point between the two fire stages. A clean filter stops grease from entering the plenum; a saturated filter becomes a fuel source directly above the appliance. Filter cleaning is therefore not housekeeping. It is the first line of fire defense in the exhaust, and it belongs on a schedule that reflects cooking volume rather than on a list of closing tasks.

## 4. What separates a contained kitchen fire from a building fire?

Time and fuel continuity separate the two. A pan fire that is covered or suppressed within the first minute consumes the oil in the pan and stops. A pan fire that reaches the hood film within the first two or three minutes has found a fuel bed that extends to the roof, and from that point the incident is governed by the building rather than by the kitchen.

The first minute belongs to the kitchen staff. The correct actions are to shut off the heat source, cover the vessel if a lid is within reach, and discharge a Class K extinguisher at the base of the fire from a safe distance. Water is not an option, because water turns to steam inside hot oil and ejects burning grease across the room. A wet chemical suppression system, where installed, discharges automatically or manually and is designed to saponify the fat, which is a different mechanism from cooling.

The second minute belongs to the suppression system and the fuel load. If the hood and duct are coated, the fire has somewhere to go. If they are clean to bare metal, the fire has very little to consume beyond the filter and the immediate plenum surface, and the suppression discharge has a realistic chance of stopping it. This is the operational meaning of cleaning to a verifiable standard: it removes the bridge between the appliance and the building.

The third minute belongs to the fire department. By then the relevant questions are access, water supply and whether the fire is inside a concealed space. A duct fire inside a ceiling or a shaft is a different incident type, with different tactics and a different loss profile, including water damage, smoke damage and the business interruption that follows.

Business interruption is the part of the loss that restaurant operators underestimate. A contained pan fire may close the kitchen for a shift. A duct fire typically closes the restaurant for weeks, because the exhaust system has to be replaced or recertified, the suppression system has to be recharged and inspected, and the health authority has to clear the kitchen before service resumes. The insurance claim may cover part of that, and the documentation is what determines how much.

## 5. What does a restaurant insurer actually require for grease fire prevention?

An insurer does not underwrite cleaning. It underwrites the probability of a loss and the ability to recover after one, and cleaning records are evidence for both. The requirements that appear in commercial property programs are therefore less about housekeeping than about proof of a maintained fire protection system.

The first requirement is compliance with the applicable standard. Most commercial property and package policies for food service operations include a maintenance condition, and the practical benchmark used by underwriters is NFPA 96, the standard for ventilation control and fire protection of commercial cooking operations. A program that follows the standard and can document it is easier to insure than one that follows a housekeeping routine and hopes the inspector agrees.

The second requirement is a defined frequency. Underwriters typically want to see that the cleaning interval matches the cooking volume, not that the kitchen was cleaned at some point in the last year. A high-volume fry and wok operation on a semiannual schedule is a different risk from the same operation on a monthly schedule, and the underwriting file reflects that difference.

The third requirement is an evidence trail. The documents that show up in a loss review are the cleaning certificates, the service reports, the before-and-after photos, the suppression inspection records and the filter logs. When those documents are missing, the insurer does not conclude that the cleaning did not happen; it concludes that the insured cannot prove it did, which in claim terms is often the same outcome.

The fourth requirement is contractor qualification. Insurers and brokers increasingly ask whether the cleaning vendor carries liability and workers compensation coverage, whether technicians are screened, and whether the vendor can produce a certificate on request. A restaurant that uses an uninsured crew to clean a grease-laden duct has transferred the cleaning cost but retained the liability.

| What the underwriter asks | Evidence that answers it | Common gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the exhaust cleaned to a recognized standard? | Certificate referencing NFPA 96 and the scope covered | Certificate names the hood but not the duct |
| Does the frequency match cooking volume? | Written schedule tied to the volume band | Fixed annual schedule regardless of menu |
| Was the most recent service completed? | Dated service report with technician name | Report with no date or no technician |
| Is the condition verifiable? | Before-and-after photos with timestamps | Photos of a clean hood front only |
| Is the suppression system maintained? | Suppression inspection and test records | Cleaning records without suppression records |
| Is the vendor qualified? | Insurance certificate, bonding, screening policy | Vendor unable to produce a certificate of insurance |

There is a commercial reason to close that gap before renewal. Underwriters price uncertainty, and a file with a clear cleaning history and a qualified vendor is a file with less uncertainty. ## 6. Which cleaning frequency will an underwriter accept?

The frequency an underwriter will accept is the frequency that matches the cooking volume, documented with the reasoning behind the choice. NFPA 96 organizes the obligation around three practical bands, and the band an operation falls into determines both the schedule and the amount of grease the system is expected to carry between services.

| Cooking volume band | Typical interval | Operations that fit | Documentation expectation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High volume | Monthly | Fryer lines, wok cooking, open flame, charbroilers, long service hours | Monthly certificate, photo set and filter log |
| Moderate volume | Quarterly | Full-service kitchens with heavy grill or fryer use | Quarterly certificate with duct scope |
| Low volume | Semiannual | Cafes, bakeries, oven-led menus with light grease | Semiannual certificate with hood and duct scope |
| Solid fuel | Monthly or more frequent | Wood, charcoal and other solid fuel cooking | Monthly certificate plus ash and creosote notes |

Three cautions apply before a restaurant adopts a band. The first is that the band is a function of the menu, not the square footage. A small kitchen running two fryers and a wok line at high volume belongs in the monthly band even if the room is compact. The second is that a change in menu or equipment changes the band, and the schedule has to be revised when a fryer is added or a late-night service begins. The third is that the authority having jurisdiction and the insurer can require a stricter interval than the general band, and the stricter requirement governs.

The band decision should be written down. A one-page memo that states the cooking volume, the equipment, the service hours, the chosen interval and the basis for it is a stronger underwriting document than a certificate alone, because it shows that the interval was chosen rather than inherited. That memo also protects the restaurant during a claim review, when the question is not whether the schedule was convenient but whether it was appropriate.

There is a cost dimension to the band that should be stated plainly. A monthly schedule costs more per year than a quarterly schedule, and the difference is real. The comparison that matters is not the cleaning invoice against zero; it is the cleaning invoice against the premium, the deductible and the uninsured portion of a loss. The [hidden costs of inconsistent cleaning](/insights/hidden-costs-inconsistent-commercial-cleaning-nj-ny) covers the operational side of that equation, and the arithmetic becomes straightforward once the deductible is included.

## 7. What documents must a hood cleaning contractor deliver?

A hood cleaning service that produces no document is a service that cannot be audited, insured or defended. The deliverable package should be defined in the contract, and it should be specific enough that a third party can read it without the contractor present.

The core document is the certificate of cleaning. At minimum it should identify the property and the system covered, the date and time of service, the technician or crew, the method used, the components cleaned, the condition observed before and after, any deficiencies found and the next service date. A certificate that lists only the hood and omits the duct, the fan and the plenum is a partial document, and a partial document is a liability in a claim review.

The second document is the service report. Where the certificate is the summary, the report is the detail: which access panels were opened, what was found inside the duct, what chemistry was used, how long the work took, and what was left out of scope and why. This is the document that answers the question an investigator asks six months later, which is what the inside of the system looked like on the day it was cleaned.

The third document is the photographic record. Photos should cover each cleaned component before and after, from the same angle, with the date and time visible. The photo set is the only part of the package that can be evaluated by someone who was not there, and it is the part most often missing when a claim is contested.

The fourth document is the deficiency notice. A cleaning crew that finds cracked filters, failed hinges, missing access panels, grease-soaked insulation or a fan that does not run should report it in writing on the day of service. Deficiencies that are reported and not corrected are a maintenance decision; deficiencies that are never reported become the contractor's problem in a loss review.

| Document | Who produces it | What it proves | Typical retention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Certificate of cleaning | Cleaning contractor | Scope, date, method and next due date | Life of the policy plus three years |
| Service report | Crew supervisor | Condition observed and work performed | Three years |
| Before-and-after photos | Technician | Verifiable condition change | Three years |
| Deficiency notice | Crew supervisor | Known defects were disclosed | Three years |
| Filter log | Kitchen or contractor | Filter cleaning and replacement history | Two years |
| Suppression inspection record | Licensed suppression contractor | System was tested and maintained | Life of the system |
| Insurance and bonding certificate | Cleaning contractor | Vendor qualification | Current at all times |

The retention column is a working default, not a legal rule. The applicable period depends on the policy, the jurisdiction and the statute of limitations for the claim type, and a restaurant should confirm the number with its broker rather than assume it. What matters operationally is that the retention period is written down and that the files survive staff turnover.

## 8. How do you prove the cleaning interval was met?

You prove the interval was met with a schedule, an execution record and a gap explanation that reconcile to each other. Any two of those three are insufficient. A schedule without execution is a plan; execution without a schedule is a story; and both without a gap explanation are a claim waiting to be challenged.

The schedule should be a controlled document. It states the interval, the properties covered, the system components in scope and the responsible party. It should be versioned, because a schedule that changes without a record of the change cannot be used to prove anything about last year.

The execution record is the certificate and service report from each visit. The useful test is not whether the documents exist but whether they reconcile: does every scheduled visit have a matching certificate, and does every certificate correspond to a scheduled visit. Most documentation failures appear in the mismatch, not in the absence.

The gap explanation is the part most restaurants skip. When a visit is missed, rescheduled or performed outside the interval, the file should say so, state the reason and state the corrective action. A missed month followed by a documented recovery is a maintenance event. A missed month followed by silence is an admission.

Three practical controls keep the interval defensible.

1. **Anchor the schedule to the calendar year, not to the invoice cycle.** A schedule that resets with each contract year drifts, and drift is what an investigator looks for.
2. **Record the actual service date, not the planned date.** A certificate dated to the planned visit is worse than no certificate, because it is inaccurate evidence.
3. **Reconcile quarterly.** One person, one hour, four times a year, matching schedule to certificates. This is the cheapest control in the entire program and the one most often missing.

A missed interval is not automatically a coverage problem. It becomes one when the restaurant cannot show that the miss was identified, corrected and followed by a return to the standard. Insurers and adjusters distinguish between a program with an exception and a program with no program, and the gap explanation is the document that draws that distinction.

## 9. How should photos and video be captured so they hold up in a claim?

Photos hold up in a claim when they answer three questions without interpretation: what was the condition, where exactly was it, and when was it recorded. A photograph that answers only the first question is a marketing image, not evidence.

The capture protocol should be written into the cleaning scope. Each component gets a wide shot that establishes location, a medium shot that shows the component in context and a close shot that shows surface condition. The same three angles are repeated after cleaning, from the same position, so the pair can be compared directly. A before photo from the floor and an after photo from a ladder is not a comparison.

Metadata matters more than most crews realize. Modern phones embed a timestamp and often a location in the file, and that metadata is what turns a photo into a record. Stripping it by sending images through a messaging app is a common and avoidable error. The original files should be retained, not screenshots of them.

Video is useful for the parts a still cannot show: the interior of a long horizontal run, the condition behind a fan, the extent of a coating on a plenum ceiling. A slow, continuous pass with the light on the surface is more persuasive than a series of disconnected stills, provided the audio or a slate identifies the property and the date.

## 10. What does the fire inspector check that the insurer also reads?

The fire inspector and the underwriter are asking the same question from different directions: is the fire protection system in the condition it is required to be in. The inspector verifies it on the day of the visit; the underwriter verifies it through the file. Where the two overlap, the cleaning record is the shared evidence.

The inspector checks the hood and duct condition, the filters, the clearance around cooking equipment, the suppression system and its current inspection tag, the extinguisher inventory and the access to concealed components. The inspector also checks whether the cleaning interval matches the cooking volume, because the interval is part of the standard rather than a suggestion.

The underwriter reads the same facts through documents: the certificate, the suppression inspection record, the extinguisher service record and the deficiency log. An inspector who finds a dirty duct writes a violation; an underwriter who finds no record of cleaning adjusts the premium, adds an exclusion or declines the risk. The restaurant experiences the first as a re-inspection and the second as a renewal conversation, and both trace to the same missing document.

The overlap also defines what good documentation looks like. A record that would satisfy an inspector, meaning it names the component, the condition and the date, will generally satisfy an underwriter. A record written for marketing, meaning it states that service was completed, will satisfy neither.

## 11. How should kitchen staff be trained to prevent and respond to grease fires?

Training has two halves, and restaurants usually invest in one. The response half teaches staff what to do when a pan ignites. The prevention half teaches them what to notice before it does. A program with only the first half produces confident staff in a kitchen that keeps catching fire.

Prevention training is about observation. Staff should know where grease accumulates, what a saturated filter looks like, how to read the suppression gauge, why the hood should never be operated with missing filters, and what to do when they see grease dripping from a duct seam. These are five-minute checks that belong in the closing routine, and they catch most developing problems before a service.

Response training is about sequence and restraint. The sequence is heat off, cover if possible, Class K extinguisher at the base, evacuate and call. The restraint is not using water, not carrying a burning pan through the dining room, and not assuming a fire is out because the flame disappeared. Grease re-ignites, and a fire that appears extinguished on the surface can flash back when air reaches it.

| Training topic | Who needs it | Frequency | Evidence of training |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grease accumulation points and daily checks | All kitchen staff | Onboarding and annually | Signed training sheet |
| Filter removal, inspection and replacement | Line cooks and closers | Quarterly | Competency sign-off |
| Class K extinguisher use | All kitchen staff | Annually with hands-on | Practical evaluation record |
| Suppression system manual pull and shutdown | Managers and leads | Semiannual | Drill log |
| Gas and equipment shutdown sequence | Managers and leads | Semiannual | Drill log |
| Evacuation and 911 protocol | All staff | Annually | Drill record with date |
| Post-incident reporting | Managers | Annually | Report template review |

The evidence column is the point. Training that is not recorded is training that did not happen, both for the insurer and for the fire department after an incident. A one-page sheet with names, dates and topics is sufficient; a folder of slides with no attendance record is not.

## 12. What belongs in a written grease fire response plan?

A response plan is a one-page document that tells a sixteen-year-old line cook what to do in the first sixty seconds. If it is longer than a page, it will not be read; if it is not posted, it will not be found.

The plan should start with the decision. If the fire is in a pan or a fryer and is small, shut off the heat, cover the vessel if a lid is within reach, and discharge a Class K extinguisher at the base. If the fire has reached the hood, the filters or the duct, do not attempt to fight it from the floor. Pull the suppression system if there is a manual station, shut off the gas, evacuate and call 911.

The plan should name the shutdown points. Gas main, electrical disconnects, suppression manual pull, and the location of the Class K extinguishers. A plan that says "shut off the gas" without saying where the valve is assumes knowledge that new staff do not have.

The plan should define who does what. One person calls 911, one person accounts for staff and guests, one person meets the fire department at the entrance with the suppression and cleaning records. The last role is frequently omitted and frequently decisive, because the first question a fire officer asks is what is inside the duct.

The plan should include the do-not list, because restraint is trainable and improvisation is not. Do not use water. Do not move a burning pan. Do not open a hood or duct access panel during a fire. Do not re-enter the kitchen until the fire department clears it. Do not restart equipment until the suppression system has been recharged and the kitchen has been cleared.

The plan should be posted, drilled and dated. A plan that exists in a binder in an office is a document; a plan that is posted at the pass and drilled twice a year is a capability. The drill record is also useful evidence, because it shows the restaurant prepared rather than reacted.

## 13. What happens after a suppression system discharges?

A suppression discharge is a controlled event with an uncontrolled aftermath. The fire may be out, but the kitchen is not operational, and the sequence that follows determines how quickly it reopens and how the claim is handled.

The first step is confirmation. The fire department confirms the fire is out and the space is safe to enter. Nobody re-enters before that, regardless of how minor the discharge appears.

The second step is documentation, and it should happen before cleanup begins. Photograph the affected area, the discharged nozzles, the suppression tag, the extinguisher used and the condition of the hood and duct. Record the time of the discharge, the cause, who was present and what was done. This record is the basis of the insurance notification and, if the cause is disputed, the basis of the defense.

The third step is notification. The insurer or broker should be notified promptly, within the period stated in the policy, and the notification should include the preliminary cause and the documentation collected. Late notice is one of the most common reasons a claim is reduced, and it is entirely avoidable.

The fourth step is the suppression recharge. A discharged system is not a protected system until a licensed contractor recharges it, replaces the fusible links and certifies the installation. Cooking cannot resume before that certification, and the health authority will generally require it as well.

The fifth step is the reopen decision. It should be made against a checklist rather than against pressure from the owner: suppression certified, exhaust cleaned and inspected, gas system checked, health authority cleared, staff briefed. A kitchen that reopens without that checklist is a kitchen that has converted one incident into two.

## 14. What happens to a claim when documentation is missing?

The claim does not automatically fail, but the burden shifts. When the insured cannot produce records showing that the exhaust was maintained, the adjuster has to decide whether the loss is covered under the policy or excluded for lack of maintenance, and that decision is made with less information than the restaurant could have provided.

Three outcomes are common. The first is a reservation of rights, where the insurer pays under protest while it investigates whether a maintenance condition applies. The second is a partial denial, where the loss is covered but the portion attributable to neglected maintenance is excluded. The third is subrogation, where the insurer pays the restaurant and then pursues the cleaning contractor or another party for the loss.

Subrogation is the outcome restaurant operators understand least and should understand best. If the insurer can show that the fire spread because the duct was not cleaned to the contracted standard, it can pursue the contractor. If it can show that the restaurant failed to schedule the cleaning it contracted for, it can reduce the payment. The file determines which theory is available.

The contract with the cleaning vendor matters here as well. Indemnity, insurance requirements and a clear scope definition determine whether the vendor's policy responds and whether the restaurant's policy has to. A vendor agreement that says "hood cleaning" without defining the duct, the fan and the plenum leaves the most expensive part of the system outside the scope and outside the indemnity.

## 15. How do you audit the grease fire program every quarter?

A quarterly audit is the control that keeps the program from decaying between renewals. It does not need to be long. It needs to be repeatable, owned by one person and documented.

Run these twelve checks in order.

1. Is the cooking volume band still accurate for the current menu and equipment?
2. Does the cleaning schedule match the band, and has it been revised since the last menu change?
3. Does every scheduled visit have a matching certificate, and does every certificate have a matching visit?
4. Does each certificate name the hood, the filters, the plenum, the duct and the fan?
5. Is there a before-and-after photo set for each component, with timestamps?
6. Are deficiencies from the last four visits logged, and are they closed?
7. Is the suppression inspection current, and is the tag visible?
8. Are the Class K extinguishers in place, charged and within service date?
9. Is the gas shutdown point marked and known to at least two managers?
10. Has the response plan been drilled in the last six months, with a record?
11. Has staff training been refreshed since the last new hire or equipment change?
12. Is the historical file stored somewhere the restaurant controls, independent of the vendor?

Each failed item is a finding, and each finding should have an owner and a date. The audit is not a compliance ritual; it is the mechanism that keeps the file current, and the file is what protects the restaurant when the program is tested.

The cost of running the audit is a few hours per year. The cost of skipping it is discovered at the worst possible moment, which is after a fire, when the restaurant needs its file to be complete and finds that it is not. ## FAQ

**How do grease fires start in a restaurant?**
Cooking oil is heated past its smoke point and then its fire point, and the vapor above the surface ignites from a burner, a hot element or a pilot. Once the oil is burning, the fire spreads to the grease film on the hood, the filters and the duct.

**What is the most common cause of a restaurant grease fire?**
Unattended cooking on a fryer, a grill or a range is the usual trigger, and accumulated grease in the exhaust is what turns a small fire into a building fire. The two causes work together, which is why cleaning and cooking discipline are both part of prevention.

**How often should a restaurant clean its hood to satisfy an insurer?**
The interval should match the cooking volume: monthly for high-volume fryer, wok and open flame operations, quarterly for moderate kitchens and semiannual for light menus. The insurer and the authority having jurisdiction can require a stricter interval, and the stricter requirement governs.

**What documents should a hood cleaning company provide?**
A certificate of cleaning, a service report, before-and-after photos with timestamps, a deficiency notice and the next service date. The certificate should name every component cleaned, including the hood, the filters, the plenum, the duct and the fan.

**Does restaurant insurance cover grease fire damage?**
Most commercial property policies cover fire damage, but coverage can be reduced or denied when the insured cannot show that the exhaust was maintained. The cleaning file is what determines how the claim is handled.

**Can you put water on a grease fire?**
No. Water turns to steam inside hot oil and throws burning grease outward, which spreads the fire and causes serious burns. Use a Class K extinguisher, cover the vessel if possible, and let the suppression system do its job.

**What should staff do first when a grease fire starts?**
Shut off the heat, cover the vessel if a lid is within reach, and discharge a Class K extinguisher at the base of the fire. If the fire has reached the hood or the duct, pull the suppression system, shut off the gas, evacuate and call 911.

**How long should hood cleaning records be kept?**
A working default is the life of the policy plus three years, and some restaurants keep records longer. The applicable period depends on the policy and the jurisdiction, so confirm the number with your broker and write it into the program.

**What happens if a hood cleaning visit is missed?**
The miss should be documented with the reason and the corrective action, and the service should be rescheduled immediately. A documented exception is a maintenance event; an undocumented gap is what an adjuster treats as evidence of neglect.

**How much does hood cleaning cost in New Jersey?**
Pricing depends on the cooking volume band, the length and access of the duct, the number of fans and the frequency. The [commercial cleaning pricing guide](/insights/commercial-cleaning-cost-nj-pricing-guide-2026) covers what drives the number and how the line item fits into a full program.
