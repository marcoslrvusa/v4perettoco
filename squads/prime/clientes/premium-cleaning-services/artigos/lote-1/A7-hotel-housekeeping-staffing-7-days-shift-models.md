> **Direct answer:** Seven-day housekeeping coverage without structural overtime requires roughly 1.4 employees per daily seat, because a five-day attendant covers five of seven days. Three models work in practice: staggered five-two crews, four-three compressed shifts, and a core crew with a dedicated weekend team. The model with the smallest headcount is almost never the cheapest.

## Key takeaways

- One daily housekeeping seat requires 1.4 employees to be covered seven days a week; budgeting one attendant per seat guarantees 28% to 30% overtime every week.
- A 160-room select-service property at 78% occupancy generates about 690 room credits and 50 eight-hour shifts of room work per week, spread unevenly from seven to nine attendants per day.
- Staffing seven attendants with overtime instead of ten on staggered schedules costs roughly $75,000 more per year on the same 20,800 hours.
- Four-ten compressed schedules give three days off but raise the coverage multiplier to 1.75, which is why they fit peak-season blocks better than year-round operation.
- Overtime above 5% of hours, a sixth consecutive working day, and absence clustering on Mondays are the earliest signals that a schedule is about to fail.

## 1. Why is seven-day coverage the hardest scheduling problem in housekeeping?

Hotels sell rooms every day, so housekeeping has to be staffed every day. Most other departments can be built around a five-day business week: the sales office, the accounting team, the engineering workshop. Floors cannot. A Sunday departure is the same work as a Tuesday departure, and the room has to be released before the arrival window regardless of the calendar.

The difficulty is arithmetic, not attitude. An attendant working five days covers five sevenths of a daily seat. Keeping one seat filled seven days a week therefore takes 1.4 employees, not one. That multiplier is the most misunderstood number in housekeeping budgeting, and it is where structural overtime is born: a property budgets seven attendants for seven daily seats, then pays the missing 2.8 positions as overtime every week of the year.

There is a second layer. Coverage is not flat across the week. Arrival and departure curves create days that need nine attendants and days that need seven. A schedule built on the weekly average will be short on the heavy days and overstaffed on the light ones, and the shortfall is always resolved the same way — with the people who are already there.

Everything below works through both layers using one reference property: a 160-room select-service hotel at 78% occupancy, generating roughly 690 room credits and 50 eight-hour shifts of room work per week. Credits follow the standard convention, where a checkout is worth 1.0 and a light stayover about 0.5, and the property delivers about 14 credits per eight-hour shift.

## 2. What does structural overtime actually cost?

Overtime has two costs, and hotels usually only see the first. The visible cost is the premium: under the Fair Labor Standards Act, hours beyond 40 in a workweek are paid at one and a half times the regular rate. The invisible cost is that structural overtime is a permanent condition disguised as an exception, so it never gets budgeted, never gets reviewed and never gets fixed.

The math is simple enough to settle any argument. If a property needs 400 hours of room work per week and employs seven full-time attendants, those seven cover 280 hours. The remaining 120 hours — 30% of the total — are overtime. On a straight-time rate of $18 an hour, that premium alone is 120 hours × $9 × 52 weeks, or roughly $56,000 a year in premium on top of the base wages the property would have paid anyway. Loaded with payroll taxes and benefits, the gap between the lean-looking seven-person model and a correctly sized ten-person model reaches about $75,000.

Three secondary costs follow, and they are where the real damage sits.

**Turnover.** Attendants on permanent overtime leave. Replacing an hourly room attendant commonly costs a few thousand dollars in recruiting, onboarding and lost productivity, and a floor that runs 30% overtime loses people faster than a floor that runs none.

**Quality drift.** The last rooms of a ten-hour day get less attention than the first, and the [hidden costs of inconsistent cleaning](/insights/hidden-costs-inconsistent-commercial-cleaning-nj-ny) show up as rework, complaints and rooms released late — none of which appear on the overtime line.

**Coverage fragility.** A schedule that depends on everyone working six days has no slack. One resignation converts a 30% overtime problem into a 45% one, and the property starts buying agency labor at 1.6 to 2.0 times the internal rate.

## 3. How many attendants does a peak arrival day require?

The peak day is not the day with the most occupied rooms. It is the day with the most work, and work is driven by departures. A departure requires stripping, a full linen change, a bathroom deep clean, vacuuming, dusting and amenity reset. A stayover requires a fraction of that.

The reference property illustrates the gap. On its heaviest day it carries 92 departures and 48 stayovers: 92 credits plus 24 credits, or 116 credits. At 14 credits per eight-hour shift, that is 8.3 shifts, rounded up to nine attendants plus a floor supervisor. On its lightest day it carries 40 departures and 94 stayovers: 87 credits, or 6.2 shifts, rounded to seven.

Two operational conclusions follow, and they contradict the way many properties schedule.

**Staff to departures, not to occupancy.** A property at 95% occupancy with few departures needs fewer attendants than a property at 75% occupancy on a turnover-heavy Monday. Occupancy is a revenue metric; departures are a labor metric.

**Round up, then flex down.** Shifts are discrete. 8.3 shifts means nine people, and the 0.7 shift of slack is where deep cleaning, public area detail and linen room work get absorbed. A property that rounds down and then asks the crew to "finish early" is scheduling 45 minutes of unpaid pressure into every heavy day. The [hotel turnover cleaning checklist](/hotel-turnover-cleaning-checklist) is the document that keeps those rounded-up hours productive rather than idle.

## 4. Which shift models cover seven days without overtime?

There are five coverage patterns in common use in the US market, and they differ in coverage, overtime exposure and the kind of property they fit. None is universally correct; each trades headcount against schedule appeal.

| Model | Pattern | Days covered | Employees per seat | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed five-two | 5 × 8 h, fixed days off | 5 of 7 | 1.40 | Flat year-round demand |
| Staggered five-two | 5 × 8 h, spread start days | 5 of 7 | 1.40 | Weekend volume |
| Compressed four-three | 4 × 10 h | 4 of 7 | 1.75 | Peak season, leisure |
| Core plus weekend team | 5 × 8 h core, weekend crew | 5 core, 2 weekend | 1.40 + 1.00 | Sharp weekend peaks |
| Split shift | Two 4–5 h blocks | 5 of 7 | 1.40 | Turndown only |

The distinction between fixed and staggered five-two is the one that decides most outcomes. Both carry the same 1.4 multiplier. A fixed model puts everyone's days off on Tuesday and Wednesday, which means Saturday and Sunday are covered by overtime every single week. A staggered model moves start days across Monday through Sunday so that the same 50 weekly shifts are distributed without a single overtime hour.

That is the whole trick, and it costs nothing. It does require two things properties resist: publishing schedules three weeks ahead, and accepting that some attendants will not have Saturday and Sunday off together. The [hotel housekeeping services](/hotel-housekeeping-services) model solves the second by hiring explicitly for weekend availability and paying a modest differential rather than paying a premium on hours already worked.

## 5. How does a staggered five-two model work in practice?

A staggered five-two schedule is built backwards from the day-of-week demand curve. The sequence has five steps, and skipping any of them produces a schedule that looks balanced on paper and breaks on Thursday.

**Step one: calculate shifts per day.** Divide daily credits by the credits-per-shift standard. The reference property needs 8.3, 6.6, 6.3, 6.5, 7.6, 6.2 and 7.9 shifts across the week.

**Step two: round each day up.** Nine, seven, seven, seven, eight, seven and eight. That is 53 shifts, not 50, because rounding absorbs the fractional demand. The three extra shifts are the property's capacity for detail work.

**Step three: assign seats, not people.** A seat is a recurring daily position. Monday needs nine seats, Tuesday seven, and so on. The maximum is nine, so the property has nine core seats plus four part-time or floating seats that only appear on heavy days.

**Step four: place days off so that each seat is covered five days.** With nine core seats at 1.4 employees each, the property needs 12.6 people — call it 13, of which three or four work 24 to 32 hours. Every seat has a named backup before the week starts.

**Step five: publish three weeks ahead and freeze.** The schedule becomes a contract. Changes after the freeze go through a swap board, not through a supervisor's phone call at 6 am.

A worked placement makes the pattern concrete. Thirteen attendants, of whom nine are full-time, each receive two consecutive days off, and the pairs are distributed so that every day of the week loses between one and two people rather than four. Monday's nine seats are filled by seven attendants whose weekend ended Sunday, one returning from a Tuesday-Wednesday pair and one from a Wednesday-Thursday pair. Saturday's seven seats are filled by attendants whose days off fall on Monday-Tuesday and Tuesday-Wednesday. No one works six days, and the property never buys a weekend hour at time and a half.

The output is a property that covers seven days with zero scheduled overtime and a known, small float. The float is the cost of the model, and it is cheaper than the overtime it replaces. What the model demands in exchange is administrative discipline: someone has to own the schedule, and that person needs authority over hiring and hours.

## 6. When does a four-three compressed schedule make sense?

The four-ten schedule — four days of ten hours, three days off — is the most requested pattern in housekeeping interviews and the least efficient pattern for seven-day coverage. Both facts are true, and the property that ignores either one gets a schedule that recruits well and budgets badly.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. An employee on four tens covers four of seven days, so a daily seat requires 1.75 employees instead of 1.40. For the reference property's nine seats, that is 15.75 people rather than 12.6 — three additional hires, each with onboarding, uniform and training cost, to deliver the same 50 shifts.

What the model does deliver is retention. Three consecutive days off is a genuine quality-of-life improvement, and properties in markets where room attendants are scarce report that the compressed schedule is the single most effective recruiting lever available. It also concentrates the crew into longer blocks, which reduces handover loss on heavy turnover days: a ten-hour attendant cleans more rooms in one continuous run than two eight-hour attendants split by a shift change.

The practical rule is to use it surgically rather than universally. Four-ten fits three situations: a peak season block of eight to twelve weeks, a property with a long-weekend leisure pattern where Monday and Tuesday are genuinely light, and a night or turndown shift where a longer continuous block reduces walking. It fits poorly in a corporate-transient property with a flat Monday-to-Friday base, where the extra headcount buys nothing.

## 7. How do you staff a core crew with a dedicated weekend team?

The third model separates the week from the weekend instead of trying to balance them. A core crew of full-time attendants covers Monday through Friday. A dedicated weekend team — often part-time, often composed of people who want exactly Saturday and Sunday — covers the two days the core crew does not.

The appeal is honesty about the labor market. Many good room attendants cannot work weekends for family reasons, and many reliable weekend workers do not want full-time hours. A single schedule that pretends both groups are interchangeable produces the worst of both: weekday staff forced into weekend overtime and weekend staff hired as full-time and then released early.

The economics depend entirely on the weekend differential. In the reference property, the weekend requires 15 shifts. Staffing those with a part-time crew at a $2 hourly differential costs more per hour than the internal rate but avoids both the overtime premium and the 1.4 multiplier on those seats. Staffing them with core-crew overtime costs 1.5 times the base rate plus the retention damage of a six-day week.

Three design rules keep this model from becoming a two-tier workforce.

**Pay the differential openly.** A weekend rate that is published and applied to everyone on the weekend shift is defensible. A quiet arrangement where weekend staff earn less per hour than weekday staff is a turnover machine.

**Give the weekend team a supervisor.** Weekend quality collapses when the weekend crew has no dedicated supervision. The reference property needs one weekend floor supervisor for 15 shifts, and that role is the difference between a model and a gap.

**Rotate one weekend per month into the core crew.** A small rotation keeps weekday staff competent on weekend patterns and gives the weekend team coverage for vacations. Without it, the weekend team becomes a separate operation with its own standards.

## 8. How should headcount be dimensioned by day of week?

Headcount planning fails when it is done monthly. A monthly average of 7.1 shifts per day describes a property that does not exist on any single day of the month. The unit of planning has to be the day, and the day has to be built from departures and stayovers.

| Day | Departures | Stayovers | Credits | Shifts at 14 credits | Attendants scheduled |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | 92 | 48 | 116 | 8.3 | 9 |
| Tuesday | 54 | 76 | 92 | 6.6 | 7 |
| Wednesday | 48 | 80 | 88 | 6.3 | 7 |
| Thursday | 52 | 78 | 91 | 6.5 | 7 |
| Friday | 76 | 62 | 107 | 7.6 | 8 |
| Saturday | 40 | 94 | 87 | 6.2 | 7 |
| Sunday | 88 | 46 | 111 | 7.9 | 8 |
| **Week** | **450** | **484** | **692** | **49.4** | **53** |

Read the table as a staffing instruction, not a report. Monday and Sunday are the heavy days, and they are heavy for different reasons: Monday carries the weekend's departures, Sunday carries the week's early arrivals. Saturday is the lightest day by credits and the busiest day by guest presence, which is why Saturday staffing decisions are usually about public areas and turndown rather than rooms.

Two rules make the table usable across a year.

**Rebuild it monthly from the reservation system, not from last month's schedule.** Departure counts change with the segment mix. A property that adds a group block on Wednesday shifts three shifts from Monday to Wednesday without changing weekly credits at all.

**Hold the weekly total and flex the daily shape.** The 53 shifts are the budget. Moving a shift from Saturday to Monday is free. Adding a shift is a decision that requires a credit justification, and that single rule prevents the slow drift where every heavy day gets one more attendant and nobody notices until payroll.

## 9. What happens when an attendant calls out?

Absence is not an exception in housekeeping; it is a rate. Unplanned absence in US hotel housekeeping commonly runs 4% to 6% of scheduled shifts, higher in properties with long commutes, split shifts or a six-day norm. A property that plans 50 shifts a week should expect two to three callouts, and the schedule either has a designed answer or it has overtime.

There are four answers, in ascending order of cost.

**A float pool.** One or two attendants scheduled four days a week whose fourth day is a coverage day rather than a floor assignment. They cost real hours, and they eliminate most callout overtime.

**Cross-trained public area staff.** Attendants who can cover a floor and public area staff who can strip a room. The cross-training is cheap; the willingness to use it depends on whether supervisors are measured on their own area or on the property.

**Voluntary extra shifts at premium.** Offered first to part-time staff and the weekend team, published on a board, filled by seniority. This is overtime, but it is chosen overtime rather than imposed overtime, and chosen overtime does far less retention damage.

**Agency or temp labor.** The most expensive option at roughly 1.6 to 2.0 times the internal hourly rate, and the least reliable on quality. It belongs in the plan as a last resort with a named threshold, not as an improvisation at 7 am.

The mistake that turns absence into a crisis is planning at zero. A schedule with no float has already spent its coverage on the good weeks, and every callout is paid at 1.5 times.

The cost difference is easy to state. Covering a single eight-hour callout with overtime costs 12 straight-time hours once the premium is applied. Covering the same callout with a float attendant who was already scheduled costs eight hours, because the float was priced into the week from the start. Across two to three callouts a week and 50 weeks, the float model is cheaper by a margin that comfortably pays for the float position itself — and it does so without asking a tired attendant to stay late.

## 10. How do you cover holidays and peak season?

Holidays and peak season are the two periods when a seven-day schedule is tested, and they fail in opposite directions. Holidays remove supply: attendants with seniority take the days off, and the property that planned on 50 shifts discovers it has 38 available people. Peak season adds demand: the same property needs 60 shifts in a week and has no mechanism to buy them.

Both are solved the same way, with a calendar built a quarter ahead.

**Map the eleven federal holidays plus the local ones that matter.** For each, record the expected occupancy, the expected departure count and the expected available headcount based on last year's requests. The gap between the last two numbers is the holiday plan, and it should be visible in September, not in December.

**Set a blackout window and pay for it.** Properties that prohibit vacation during peak weeks without compensating for it lose staff. Properties that pay a holiday differential, or that offer a compensatory day in the following month, keep the crew. The differential is cheaper than the agency invoice.

**Pre-book the peak weeks with the weekend team and the float pool.** Peak season is the one period where the compressed four-ten schedule earns its extra headcount, because the demand is genuinely concentrated and the retention benefit is worth the multiplier.

**Decide the overflow policy before the overflow.** Which rooms get released late if the crew is short? Which floors get a reduced service? A property that has not decided this will decide it at 2 pm on the worst day, and the decision will be made by whoever is most tired.

The properties that handle peak season well are not the ones with the deepest bench. They are the ones that wrote the calendar in advance and priced the coverage into the annual budget instead of discovering it in the weekly variance report.

## 11. What does each coverage model cost per year?

Cost comparison is where coverage models stop being a scheduling preference and become a financial decision. The four models below deliver the identical 20,800 hours of room work per year for the reference property, at a straight-time loaded cost of $24 per hour, an overtime rate of $36 and an agency rate of $40.

| Model | Straight hours | Premium hours | Annual cost | Delta |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7 FTE, five-two, OT 2 days | 14,560 | 6,240 OT | $574,080 | +$74,880 |
| 10 FTE, staggered five-two | 20,800 | 0 | $499,200 | baseline |
| 8 FTE plus 4 part-time | 16,640 | 4,160 part-time | $507,520 | +$8,320 |
| 9 FTE plus agency | 18,720 | 2,080 agency | $532,480 | +$33,280 |

The result inverts the intuition that dominates housekeeping budget meetings. The model with the fewest full-time employees is the most expensive, by about 15%. The model with the most full-time employees is the cheapest, because it is the only one that pays straight time for every hour the property actually needs.

Three cautions before anyone takes this table to a controller.

**The baseline assumes perfect schedule construction.** Ten full-time employees cover 50 shifts only if the days off are staggered correctly. Ten employees on a fixed five-two pattern with the same days off cover 50 shifts and still create weekend overtime.

**The part-time rate is not free.** Part-time staff avoid some benefit cost but usually carry a higher hourly rate and a higher absence rate. The table uses a $26 loaded rate for that group, which is conservative in a tight market.

**The comparison excludes turnover.** If the overtime model loses two attendants a year and the staggered model loses one, the effective gap widens by several thousand dollars and by the supervisory time spent on hiring. ## 12. Which fatigue indicators predict a schedule failure?

Fatigue does not announce itself. It appears as a slow increase in rework, a cluster of Monday callouts, a resignation that surprises nobody on the floor. Six indicators, reviewed monthly, catch the drift before it becomes a staffing crisis.

| Indicator | Measurement | Warning level | Predicts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Overtime share | OT hours ÷ total hours | Above 5% | Understaffing |
| Consecutive days | Longest run without a day off | Above 5 | Absence, injury |
| Weekly hours | Hours per attendant | Above 45 | Turnover |
| Absence clustering | Callouts by weekday | Monday/Friday spike | Dissatisfaction |
| Rework rate | Rooms returned | Above 8% | Speed over quality |
| Split-shift count | Breaks over 3 hours | Rising trend | Senior attrition |

Two interpretations matter more than the thresholds.

**Overtime share above 5% is a design problem, not a discipline problem.** A property that runs 12% overtime has a schedule that does not match its demand curve. Adding pressure to the crew does not change the arithmetic; it only changes who leaves first.

**Consecutive days is the indicator hotels underuse.** An attendant working six days at eight hours is at 48 hours and inside many legal limits, but the sixth day is where rework concentrates. Some properties cap consecutive days at five regardless of weekly hours, and those properties see the difference in inspection scores within a month.

The indicators work as a set. High overtime with low rework means the crew is absorbing the load and the property is one resignation away from failure. Normal overtime with rising rework means the standard itself has drifted.

One further indicator belongs on the same page even though it is not a fatigue measure: the ratio of scheduled hours to required hours. A property that schedules 53 shifts for 50 shifts of work is carrying a 6% float, which is healthy. A property that schedules 50 shifts for 53 shifts of work is borrowing three shifts from the crew every week, and that borrowing shows up somewhere — as overtime, as rework, or as a resignation that arrives without warning. Publishing the ratio next to the overtime share turns an abstract staffing argument into a visible, weekly number that a general manager and a housekeeping director can both read.

## 13. How do you know the schedule is working?

A schedule is working when four conditions hold at the same time, and no single metric proves it. Review them in one 20-minute meeting a week, with the same numbers every week.

**Coverage was met without unplanned overtime.** Every seat filled, and overtime confined to voluntary extra shifts. If the week required imposed overtime, the schedule failed regardless of what the rooms looked like.

**Rooms were released on time.** Release lag — the interval between attendant completion and front-office availability — under 30 minutes on 95% of rooms. Late release is the symptom that shows up in guest complaints before it shows up in any labor report.

**Rework stayed inside the band.** Below 8% of inspected rooms returned. A schedule that hits coverage by compressing time will show here first.

**Absence stayed inside the plan.** Callouts within the 4% to 6% band, with no clustering on a single day. Clustering is the signature of a schedule people dislike rather than a crew that is unwell.

The meeting should review exceptions, not averages. Three floors that consistently run late and two attendants who consistently finish early are the information the average hides, and they are the raw material for next month's schedule.

It also helps to run the review in the same order every week: coverage first, release second, rework third, absence fourth. That order matters because it mirrors causality. A coverage failure explains a release failure, and a release failure explains part of the rework. Reviewing them out of order produces a conversation about people when the subject is the schedule.

## 14. When does a third-party housekeeping partner solve coverage?

Outsourcing does not fix a schedule; it transfers the coverage problem to an organization whose entire operating model is built around it. That transfer is rational under four conditions, and irrational under a fifth that properties routinely mistake for the first four.

**Occupancy swings wider than the schedule can absorb.** A seasonal property moving between 45% and 92% needs a bench in July and a lean crew in January. A vendor prices that variability as a service level; an in-house team experiences it as hiring and layoffs.

**Weekend and holiday coverage is the recurring failure point.** If the property's overtime concentrates on Saturday, Sunday and holidays, the problem is not headcount — it is that the property is trying to staff a seven-day operation with a five-day labor model.

**Recruitment is the constraint, not cost.** In markets where room attendants are genuinely scarce, a vendor with a training pipeline and multi-property coverage converts a vacancy into a fill rate.

**Pre-opening and repositioning.** A new build or a renovation needs a crew that can hit brand standards from day one and scale down after stabilization.

The fifth condition is the trap: a property with a broken credit table and an unrealistic productivity standard will outsource and receive a cheaper version of an undefined service. The vendor inherits the property's definition of a room, prices that definition, and the overtime reappears inside the contract as a change order.

Properties that outsource well do three things first. They document the credit table. They measure a representative week. They write the coverage requirement — days, hours, peak windows — into the scope. ## FAQ

**How many housekeepers do I need for a 150-room hotel?**
For a select-service property at roughly 78% occupancy, plan on 11 to 13 full-time equivalents to cover seven days, plus one or two float or part-time attendants for callouts. The number comes from daily credits divided by the credits-per-shift standard, then multiplied by 1.4 for seven-day coverage.

**What is the 1.4 multiplier in housekeeping staffing?**
It is the number of employees required to keep one daily seat filled seven days a week when each employee works five days. Seven divided by five is 1.4. Budgeting one attendant per seat instead of 1.4 guarantees that roughly 28% of the week's hours will be paid as overtime.

**Can a hotel run housekeeping seven days a week without overtime?**
Yes, if the schedule is staggered. The same 50 weekly shifts can be covered with 10 full-time employees on staggered days off, with zero overtime. The constraint is administrative: schedules must be published three weeks ahead and days off cannot all fall on the same two days.

**Is a four-day ten-hour shift better than five eight-hour shifts?**
It is better for retention and worse for coverage economics. A four-ten attendant covers four of seven days, so a daily seat needs 1.75 employees instead of 1.40. Use it for peak-season blocks, long-weekend leisure markets or night shifts rather than as a year-round default.

**How do you cover weekends in hotel housekeeping?**
Three ways work: stagger days off so some attendants are always scheduled on Saturday and Sunday, hire a dedicated part-time weekend team with a published differential and its own supervisor, or accept weekend overtime as a planned cost. The first is cheapest, the second is most stable, the third is the most expensive and the most common.

**What is an acceptable overtime rate in housekeeping?**
Below 5% of total hours is healthy and usually reflects voluntary extra shifts. Between 5% and 10% signals a schedule that does not match the demand curve. Above 10% is structural overtime, and it should be treated as a design defect rather than a payroll variance.

**How do you handle callouts in housekeeping?**
Plan for them. Unplanned absence commonly runs 4% to 6% of shifts, so a 50-shift week should expect two to three callouts. A float pool of one or two attendants, cross-trained public area staff and a published extra-shift board cover most of it without imposed overtime.

**How many room attendants should one supervisor cover?**
A practical reference is 12 to 18 attendants per floor supervisor, with inspection built into the role rather than added on top. Above that ratio, inspection becomes sampling and rework rises before anyone notices.

**What is structural overtime?**
It is overtime that recurs every week because the schedule is understaffed by design, not because of an unusual event. If a property pays the same overtime hours in a quiet week as in a sold-out week, the overtime is structural and the fix is headcount and days-off placement, not discipline.

**Is it cheaper to outsource weekend housekeeping?**
Often, yes, when the alternative is 15 shifts of weekend overtime every week. A part-time weekend crew or an outsourced weekend team typically costs less than 1.5 times the internal rate applied to the same hours, and it removes the retention damage of a permanent six-day week.
