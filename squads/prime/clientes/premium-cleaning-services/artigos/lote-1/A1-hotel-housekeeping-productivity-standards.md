> **Resposta direta:** housekeeping productivity standards measure how many room credits a room attendant completes per productive hour. Typical operating ranges run from 3,0 to 4,0 credits per hour in economy and select-service hotels, 2,2 to 3,0 in upscale, and 1,5 to 2,4 in luxury and resort properties, where a checkout can absorb 45 to 60 minutes of labor.

## Key takeaways

- A productivity standard is not a target to squeeze labor; it is the unit that lets you price a contract, schedule a shift and defend service quality with numbers.
- Rooms per attendant hour only makes sense once you define the **room credit** — the weighted value of a checkout, a stayover, a turndown and a deep clean.
- The same 120-room hotel needs 14 attendants at a 4,0 credit standard and 19 attendants at a 3,0 standard. That gap is roughly 30% of housekeeping payroll.
- Property tier, bathroom count, square footage, linen program and inspection layer move the number more than any productivity initiative.
- Brand standards and union agreements cap how far a standard can be pushed before quality or compliance breaks.

## 1. What a housekeeping productivity standard actually measures

Most hotel teams discuss productivity as a feeling: "the floor was heavy today", "we were short two attendants", "the crew is slow". A standard replaces the feeling with an arithmetic unit that can be scheduled, priced and audited.

The unit is the **room credit per productive hour**. A credit is the normalized amount of work a room represents. A departure with two beds, a full linen change and a bathroom deep clean is worth 1,0 credit. A stayover with a light refresh is worth less. A turndown is worth less still. Once each room type carries a credit value, the standard becomes a single number: credits completed divided by productive hours worked.

Two words in that sentence carry the entire discipline.

**Productive hours** excludes breaks, meal periods, pre-shift lineup, cart setup, linen room trips and end-of-shift paperwork. If a standard is calculated over paid hours instead of productive hours, it silently inflates by 10% to 18% depending on shift structure, and the property will believe it is hitting a target it is nowhere near.

**Completed** means inspected and released, not "the attendant said it was done". A standard measured on self-reported completion is a scheduling tool, not a quality tool. The inspection layer is what separates the two, and the sections below treat it as part of the standard rather than as an afterthought.

## 2. Why the standard matters more than any single efficiency project

Housekeeping is the largest controllable labor line in a hotel. Depending on tier and service model, room cleaning absorbs somewhere between half and two-thirds of total housekeeping cost, and housekeeping itself is routinely the largest department in headcount. A standard that is 0,4 credits per hour too generous on a 150-room property translates into several full-time positions that never appear in a budget review as a single line item — they appear as overtime, as agency labor, as turnover, and as rooms released late, which is where the [hidden costs of inconsistent cleaning](/insights/hidden-costs-inconsistent-commercial-cleaning-nj-ny) accumulate without ever being invoiced.

The standard also governs three decisions that hoteliers make constantly and usually make by intuition:

1. **Scheduling.** How many attendants on a Tuesday with 92 departures and 40 stayovers?
2. **Pricing.** What does it cost to clean a room, and therefore what does a contract for 150 rooms cost per month?
3. **Vendor accountability.** When a third-party crew misses rooms, is the cause volume, standard or staffing?

Without a standard, all three conversations become opinion contests. With a standard, they become arithmetic — and arithmetic is the only language in which a facilities manager and a cleaning vendor can disagree productively.

## 3. The benchmark: rooms per attendant hour by property tier

The ranges below reflect common operating practice in the United States for properties running a conventional cart-based, floor-by-floor model with an inspection layer, the model most [hotel housekeeping services](/hotel-housekeeping-services) are staffed and priced against. They are ranges, not laws: a 90-room boutique with 55 square meter rooms and turndown service will sit at the bottom of its tier, and a limited-service property with compact rooms and no F&B will sit at the top.

| Property tier | Credits per productive hour | Rooms per 8-hour shift | Typical minutes per checkout |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Economy / limited service | 3,6 – 4,2 | 15 – 17 | 22 – 28 |
| Select service | 3,0 – 3,6 | 13 – 15 | 28 – 34 |
| Full service, upscale | 2,4 – 3,0 | 11 – 13 | 34 – 42 |
| Luxury | 1,8 – 2,4 | 8 – 11 | 42 – 55 |
| Resort / large suites | 1,5 – 2,0 | 7 – 9 | 50 – 65 |

Three cautions before anyone benchmarks a property against this table.

**The table assumes a mixed inventory.** A shift of pure departures is heavier than a shift of pure stayovers. Comparing a property's Monday (heavy departures) to a benchmark built on a blended week will always look like failure.

**The table assumes no turndown.** Evening turndown adds a separate credit line of roughly 0,15 to 0,25 credit per room and is usually staffed as its own shift.

**The table assumes a functioning linen and supply flow.** An attendant who walks to a distant linen room four times per shift loses the equivalent of one to two rooms. The standard is a measure of the system, not only of the person.

## 4. Room credits: defining the unit before arguing about the number

The most common reason two hoteliers disagree about productivity is that they are counting different work. Align the credit table first; the standard follows almost automatically.

| Room status | Credit value | What it includes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Checkout, standard room | 1,00 | Strip beds, full linen change, bathroom deep clean, vacuum, dust, amenities, terrace/balcony if present |
| Checkout, suite (2+ rooms) | 1,50 – 2,00 | Per living area plus bedroom, second bathroom, kitchenette where applicable |
| Stayover, light | 0,45 – 0,55 | Beds made or refreshed, towels, trash, bathroom touch-up, vacuum high-traffic path |
| Stayover, full | 0,65 – 0,80 | Linen change on request, full bathroom clean, vacuum, dust |
| Turndown | 0,15 – 0,25 | Bed turn, curtains, trash, towel refresh, ice/water, lighting |
| Deep clean / detail | 2,00 – 3,00 | Baseboards, vents, grout, mattress rotation, upholstery, high dusting |
| Public area (per 100 m²) | 0,20 – 0,35 | Lobby, restrooms, elevators, corridors, F&B outlets |

Two operational rules make this table usable.

**Rule one: the credit table is public inside the property.** Attendants, supervisors, the front office and any vendor should see the same numbers. Standards that live only in a manager's spreadsheet become a source of distrust; standards posted on the linen room wall become a shared target.

**Rule two: credits are revised on evidence, not on pressure.** When a property adds a coffee program, a minibar restock or a sustainability card that changes linen behavior, the credit table changes. When a general manager simply wants better numbers, the credit table does not change — the staffing model does.

## 5. Building your property's standard: the formula and a worked example

Industry ranges are a starting point. Every property needs its own number, and the construction is straightforward arithmetic that a housekeeping manager, a controller and a vendor can all reproduce independently.

**Step 1 — count the credits in a representative week.** Take seven consecutive days, not a holiday week and not a group-heavy week. Convert each room status into credits using the table above.

**Step 2 — count productive hours actually worked.** Paid hours minus meal periods, breaks, lineup and non-room tasks. If the property does not track this separately, the first month of the program is a measurement month; do not set a standard on estimated productive hours.

**Step 3 — divide, then sanity-check by tier.** Credits divided by productive hours gives the achieved standard. Compare it to the tier range. A result far outside the range usually means the credit table is wrong, not that the team is exceptional.

**Step 1 in practice — how to time the seven days.** Counting room statuses for a week is easy; counting minutes is where most programs quietly fail. Time at least 20 checkouts, 15 stayovers and 10 suites per room type, across floors and both shifts. Start the clock when the attendant crosses the threshold with the cart parked and the door open; stop it when the room is presented and the attendant leaves. Cart travel, linen trips and guest interruptions go on a separate line, never inside room time; folding them in is how a property convinces itself its rooms take 48 minutes when they take 34. If a guest returns mid-clean, stop the watch, note the interruption and resume — an interrupted room is a data point about arrival patterns, not about cleaning speed.

Two rules keep the sample honest. First, supervisors do not choose the rooms; they pick the easy ones. Assign rooms from the day's board in sequence and time whatever comes. Second, time the same attendant more than once. One observation measures the room, five observations measure the attendant, twenty measure the property.

**Sampling error, and how much precision is actually needed.** Room times vary because rooms vary: a departure after a three-night stay is not the same event as a one-night business checkout. With 20 observations per room type, a property can usually resolve the average to within two or three minutes, which is enough to set a standard at 0,1 credit resolution. With five observations, the average can move eight to ten minutes on a single atypical room, and the standard will be wrong in whichever direction that room fell. Where the timing budget is limited, group by similarity: a standard king and a standard double sit within two minutes of each other, while a suite needs its own sample.

**Rooms outside the standard.** Every week produces rooms that no standard should absorb: a smoking recovery, a pet room with hair in the upholstery, a room released after a repair with drywall dust. That is real work and must be paid for, but it must not enter the average, or the standard drifts upward in a bad week. Log these as exceptions with their own credit value — typically 1,5 to 2,5 for a recovery — and report them as a separate line. Hiding exceptions inside the average produces a number the floor does not believe.

**Worked example.** A 140-room select-service property with a blended week of 620 checkouts, 240 light stayovers and 40 turndowns:

- Checkouts: 620 × 1,00 = 620,00 credits
- Stayovers: 240 × 0,50 = 120,00 credits
- Turndowns: 40 × 0,20 = 8,00 credits
- Total: 748 credits over seven days

If room attendants worked 1.870 productive hours in that week (an average of 267 hours per day across both shifts, net of breaks and lineup), the achieved standard is 748 ÷ 1.870 = **0,40 credits per productive hour per attendant-shift equivalent** — which, expressed the way the industry speaks, is 3,2 credits per 8-hour productive shift. That sits inside the select-service band and tells the property it is operating normally.

Now the useful part: the same 748 credits at the top of the band (3,6 per 8 hours, or 0,45 per hour) require 1.662 productive hours — about 11% fewer. At the bottom (3,0 per 8 hours, or 0,375 per hour) they require 1.995 hours — about 7% more. A general manager who wants to "save two positions" is really asking for a 10% standard increase, and that request should be evaluated as a change in service model, not as a scheduling tweak.

## 6. The six variables that break the benchmark

Two properties with identical room counts, identical tiers and identical occupancy can legitimately run standards 25% apart. The difference almost always comes from one of six variables.

**Room size and configuration.** Square footage drives vacuum, dust and bathroom time. A property with two bathrooms per key carries roughly 15% to 20% more checkout labor than a single-bathroom property of the same tier. Junior suites with a kitchenette add another 10% to 15% on departure.

**Linen and terry program.** A property that changes linen daily, offers four towel sets and stocks robes runs heavier than one with a conservative reuse program. This is a brand and guest-experience decision, not a housekeeping decision, and it must be priced into the standard rather than absorbed silently.

**Occupancy pattern and arrival mix.** Group blocks produce synchronized departures and arrivals, compressing work into narrow windows. A property with a 3 pm check-in guarantee and 90 departures before noon needs peak staffing that a blended average will never reveal.

**Amenity and minibar complexity.** Restocking minibars, coffee capsules, welcome amenities and turndown gifts adds minutes per room that are invisible in a rooms-per-hour ratio but very visible in payroll.

**Physical plant.** Long corridors, multiple towers, elevator bottlenecks and distant linen rooms consume time that never appears in a per-room time study. A property with a 90-second walk to linen should expect a lower credit rate and should fix the flow before blaming the crew.

**Inspection and rework rate.** When 12% of rooms fail first inspection, the effective standard drops by roughly the same proportion because failed rooms are cleaned twice. Rework is the hidden tax on a standard that looks fine on paper.

**Suites, connecting rooms and accessible rooms.** The credit table handles suites as a multiple of a standard room, which is directionally right and practically imprecise. A suite is better built as a sum: living area at roughly 0,35 to 0,45, each bedroom at 1,0, each additional bathroom at 0,30 to 0,40, and a kitchenette at 0,25 to 0,35 where the property actually stocks it. Connecting rooms cost less than two separate rooms because the attendant crosses once, but more than one because both sides are presented and both bathrooms serviced. Accessible rooms run four to eight minutes above a standard room: larger bathroom footprints, roll-in showers with floor drains, grab bars that collect water spots, and lower closet rods that change the linen routine. Individually small, together they explain most of the gap between a property at 3,4 and a visually identical one at 3,0.

Each variable can be ranked by its drag on the standard:

| Variable | Impact on credits per hour | Trigger threshold | Where it appears first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Room size above tier median | −0,05 to −0,20 per extra 10 m² | Keys above 35 m² | Checkout minutes |
| Second bathroom per key | −0,15 to −0,25 | Any key with 2+ baths | Checkout minutes, linen usage |
| Daily linen change | −0,10 to −0,20 | Brand-mandated daily change | Linen room trips, payroll |
| Suite share above 15% of inventory | −0,10 to −0,30 | Mix shift after renovation | Credits per hour only |
| Accessible rooms above 10% of keys | −0,03 to −0,08 | ADA-heavy inventory | Checkout minutes |
| Walking distance to linen | −0,10 to −0,25 | Over 60 seconds one way | Release lag |
| Rework rate | −0,10 to −0,35 | Above 8% | Rework and credits together |
| Minibar and amenity program | −0,05 to −0,15 | Restock at every service | Stayover minutes |
| Arrival compression | −0,05 to −0,15 on the weekly average | 80+ departures before noon | Overtime share |

The ranges are not additive: a second bathroom and a distant linen room cost whatever they cost in combination on that floor plan. Then time the two largest before touching the standard.

## 7. Union agreements, labor law and the limits of the number

In unionized markets, the productivity standard is not a management preference; it is frequently a negotiated term. Contracts may specify room quotas, relief periods, restrictions on combining job classifications, and procedures for workload disputes. In New York City, housekeeping workload has been a recurring subject of bargaining, and any standard set without reference to the applicable agreement is likely to fail on the floor or in grievance.

Three practical rules for properties with a collective bargaining agreement:

1. **Read the agreement before designing the program.** Quotas, rotation rules and relief language define the legal ceiling on productivity. A standard above that ceiling is not ambitious; it is a dispute waiting to be filed.
2. **Document workload data consistently.** If the property cannot show how credits and hours were calculated, every workload conversation becomes anecdotal and the union's version of events wins by default.
3. **Separate productivity from discipline.** A standard is a planning tool. Using it as the sole basis for discipline, without a quality and attendance framework, invites claims and destroys the credibility of the measurement.

For non-union properties, the same discipline applies for a different reason: state wage-and-hour rules on meal periods, travel time between buildings and pre-shift activities determine what counts as productive time, and misclassifying those hours produces both inaccurate standards and legal exposure.

## 8. Turnaround time versus quality: the inspection layer

A standard measured only in rooms per hour rewards speed and hides rework. The inspection layer closes that gap by making release conditional on quality, and it belongs in the standard rather than in a separate quality program.

A workable structure has three tiers:

| Inspection tier | Who performs it | Coverage | What it catches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-check | Room attendant | 100% of rooms, 30–60 seconds per room | Missed amenities, visible trash, bed presentation |
| Supervisor spot check | Floor supervisor | 15–25% of rooms, before release | Bathroom detail, dust lines, linen presentation |
| Manager audit | Executive housekeeper | 3–5% of rooms, weekly rotation | Systemic misses, training gaps, standard drift |

The released-room rule matters: a room is not "done" when the attendant finishes; it is done when it passes its assigned check and is released to the front office. Properties that release rooms to inventory before inspection consistently show higher guest complaint rates on cleanliness even when their productivity numbers look strong — because the standard was measuring the wrong event. Any [hotel turnover cleaning checklist](/hotel-turnover-cleaning-checklist) should therefore end on release, not on completion.

Two metrics complete the layer. **Rework rate** is the percentage of inspected rooms returned for correction. **Release lag** is the average time between attendant completion and front-office availability. A property with a 3,2 credit standard, a 6% rework rate and a 20-minute release lag is in better shape than one with a 3,6 standard, a 15% rework rate and a 45-minute lag, even though the second property will report a better productivity number in its weekly deck.

## 9. When brand standards override the local math

A property flying a major flag does not own its own cleaning standard. Marriott, Hilton, Hyatt, IHG and Accor each publish brand standards that define minimum frequencies, approved chemicals, linen change policy, room presentation and inspection documentation. A productivity standard that contradicts the brand standard will fail an audit regardless of how elegant the arithmetic is.

Three consequences follow, and they shape every vendor conversation.

**Brand standards set the floor, not the ceiling.** A brand may require daily linen change in a given tier; the standard must then price daily linen change in, even if the local market would accept reuse. Attempting to hit a productivity target by quietly reducing a brand-required step is the fastest route to a failed quality audit and, in franchise agreements, to financial penalties.

**Documentation is part of compliance.** Most brand programs require records: inspection scores, chemical logs, training records, incident reports. A third-party housekeeping vendor must be able to produce these in the brand's format, not in its own. Contracts that do not specify reporting deliverables tend to generate exactly the reports the property needs at the worst possible moment.

**Brand standards vary by tier inside the same flag.** A Courtyard and a Ritz-Carlton are not held to the same presentation standard, and their productivity ranges differ accordingly. Benchmarking across a portfolio without segmenting by brand tier produces misleading comparisons and, frequently, unfair pressure on the properties that carry the heavier standard.

The practical rule: build the credit table from the brand standard downward. Every brand-required step gets a credit line. What remains is where productivity gains are legitimately available.

**Negotiating the standard with the flag.** A property that believes its brand standard is heavier than its tier range allows has exactly one legitimate route: a documented variance request, not a quiet reduction. The file should contain the credit table, timed observations by room type, the brand-required steps that drive the difference, and the hours they consume per week. Brand compliance teams respond to arithmetic, not to "the market will not pay for this". Variances are most often granted for physical constraints — a heritage building with no service elevator, detached wings, an unusually high suite share — and almost never for cost pressure. The distinction decides the outcome: a variance argued from the building survives an audit, and a variance argued from the budget does not. Bring it before the standard is published, not after the first failed inspection, and renew it after any renovation or room-category change.

## 10. The five KPIs to run weekly

A standard that is set but not monitored decays within a quarter. Five metrics, reviewed weekly in a 20-minute meeting, keep it alive.

| KPI | Definition | Healthy range | What a bad reading means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Credits per productive hour | Total credits ÷ productive hours | Within tier band | Credit table wrong, or staffing mismatch |
| Checkout minutes | Average minutes per checkout | Within tier range | Training gap, room complexity, or flow problem |
| Rework rate | % of inspected rooms returned | Below 8% | Inspection too lenient, or training insufficient |
| Release lag | Minutes from completion to availability | Under 30 min | Inspection bottleneck or supervisor coverage |
| Overtime share | OT hours ÷ total hours | Below 5% | Standard too tight for arrival peaks |

Two notes on interpretation. First, these metrics are only meaningful together. A low checkout time with a high rework rate is not efficiency; it is speed purchased with quality. Second, the meeting should review exceptions, not averages: averages hide the three floors that are consistently late and the two attendants who are consistently ahead.

## 11. Diagnosing a miss: the decision sequence

When a property misses its standard for two consecutive weeks, the instinct is to add staff or push the crew. Both are usually wrong. Work the sequence in order.

1. **Is the credit table still accurate?** New room type, new amenity, new linen program, renovation. If yes, revise credits and re-baseline.
2. **Is productive time being measured correctly?** Lineup, breaks, linen trips and cart setup creeping into the productive bucket will make a healthy operation look inefficient.
3. **Is the arrival pattern the cause?** Compare the miss to departure concentration. A miss confined to days with more than 80 departures is a scheduling problem, not a productivity problem.
4. **Is rework consuming capacity?** Pull the inspection data. A rising rework rate silently removes hours from the productive pool.
5. **Is the physical flow the bottleneck?** Time the walk to linen, the elevator wait and the cart turnaround. Flow problems masquerade as slow teams.
6. **Is it a training or supervision gap?** Only after the first five are eliminated does the conversation move to individual performance.

This sequence matters commercially as much as operationally: it prevents a property from renegotiating a contract, or replacing a vendor, over a problem that lives in its own scheduling or inventory data.

## 12. When a third-party housekeeping partner makes sense

Outsourcing housekeeping is not a productivity decision by itself; it is a risk-transfer decision. It becomes rational under a recognizable set of conditions.

- **Occupancy volatility.** Seasonal properties that swing between 45% and 92% occupancy carry a staffing problem that a fixed crew cannot solve without sustained overtime.
- **Recruitment and retention friction.** When the local labor market cannot supply reliable room attendants, a vendor with a bench, a training pipeline and multi-property coverage converts a chronic vacancy problem into a service level.
- **Pre-opening or repositioning.** New builds and renovations need a crew that can scale in weeks, hit brand standards from day one and then step back.
- **Owner-operator tension.** When ownership wants cost certainty and management wants flexibility, a contract with a defined credit standard and clear KPIs turns an argument into a service level agreement — and a [hospitality cleaning partner in New Jersey](/hospitality-cleaning-services-nj) that quotes against a published standard can be compared with an in-house model line by line.
- **Coverage gaps.** Weekend, holiday and emergency coverage is where in-house teams break first and where a vendor's depth is most visible.

The counterweight: outsourcing does not fix a broken credit table. A vendor inherits the property's definition of a room and will price that definition. Properties that outsource without a credit table usually discover, in month three, that they have purchased a cheaper version of an undefined service.

## 13. A 30-day implementation plan

**Week 1 — Measure.** Build the credit table, audit ten rooms with a stopwatch across each room type, and record productive versus paid hours for seven consecutive days. No targets this week.

**Week 2 — Baseline and calibrate.** Calculate the achieved standard. Compare with the tier band. Identify the two largest gaps from the six variables and quantify their cost in hours.

**Week 3 — Set and communicate.** Publish the standard, the credit table and the inspection tiers. Brief attendants, supervisors and the front office in the same meeting, with the same numbers. Set the weekly KPI review.

**Week 4 — Operate and adjust.** Run the standard live. Expect a 5% to 10% dip in the first days as the team adapts to inspection discipline. After two full weeks, adjust credits where the stopwatch data disagrees with the assumption, and only then consider staffing changes.

The most common failure is compressing these four weeks into one and calling the result a standard. A number produced without measurement is a target, and targets without measurement are how housekeeping departments end up with permanent overtime and a rotating cast of vendors.

## 14. How the credit table becomes a contract price

A productivity standard only proves itself when it survives commercial translation. The bridge is simple: credits per month, hours per month, loaded cost per hour, price.

Take the earlier example, now at monthly scale. A 140-room property with 3.100 credits per month at a 3,2-credit standard per 8-hour shift needs 969 shift-equivalents, or roughly 7.750 productive hours. Add 15% non-productive hours (lineup, breaks, linen) and you land at approximately 8.900 paid hours.

On top of that number sit payroll burden, uniforms, training, supervision, supplies and equipment. This is where two proposals that look identical separate: one may be using 3,6 credits per shift (fewer hours, more pressure) and the other 3,0 (more hours, more slack). Without the credit table attached to the contract, the client has no way to know which of the two it bought. The [housekeeping cost and pricing guide](/hotel-housekeeping-cost-pricing-guide-2026) works through the same arithmetic line by line.

Three clauses make the contract verifiable:

1. **Credit definition attached to the contract**, with values by room status and by suite type.
2. **Declared productivity standard**, with a tolerance band and a measurement methodology.
3. **Quality KPIs tied to payment**, with rework and release lag reported monthly.

Contracts without these three clauses are usually renegotiated on perception, and renegotiation on perception always favors whoever holds the data — which is rarely the client.

## 15. The five documents a hotel should demand from its partner

Before signing with an outsourced floor crew, ask for five documents. They are not bureaucracy: each one corresponds to a risk that, without a document, migrates to the hotel.

| Document | What it proves | Risk it eliminates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Certificate of insurance (liability + workers comp) | Current coverage and limits | Vicarious liability in an accident |
| Bonding and background check policy | Personnel screening | Unauthorized access to guest areas |
| Brand-specific training plan | Compliance with the brand standard | Failed quality audit |
| Inspection methodology and reporting | Ability to measure what it delivers | No evidence in a dispute |
| Credit table and schedule | Pricing transparency | Purchase of an undefined service |

A serious partner delivers all five within a week and discusses the numbers openly. A partner that hesitates to show the credit table is, in practice, selling labor availability — not a service standard; the [commercial cleaning pricing guide for New Jersey](/insights/commercial-cleaning-cost-nj-pricing-guide-2026) shows what that difference looks like inside a proposal.


## 16. Turndown, F&B and properties with night service

Properties that run turndown and food service have a floor standard that does not fit the daytime average. The night shift is shorter, more fragmented and more exposed to interruption — a guest in the room, dinner service in progress, elevators shared with room service.

In practice, the night standard should be calculated separately, with its own credits and a lower conversion rate. A working reference: the turndown shift typically delivers between 2,0 and 3,0 credits per hour, depending on how many rooms are actually occupied and how concentrated the inventory is. In properties with long wings, walking consumes a disproportionate share of the shift.

Three adjustments keep the night shift from distorting the overall standard:

- **Separate the dashboards.** Day and night have their own credits, targets and KPIs. Blending the two hides inefficiency in one and overestimation in the other.
- **Route by proximity.** Turndown is sensitive to walking. Grouping rooms by wing cuts minutes per room more than any speed incentive.
- **Treat F&B as an internal client with a deadline.** Cleaning restaurants and bars after service runs against a fixed window — the same discipline that governs [overnight restaurant cleaning](/overnight-restaurant-cleaning) in standalone accounts; if it slips, the morning floor shift starts late. The night standard has to reserve that capacity, not improvise it.

## 17. Checklist for auditing a standard already in operation

Run this list every quarter. Every failed item is a source of error in the number you report to ownership.

1. Is the credit table attached to the contract or the internal manual, and is it the same one used on the schedule?
2. Are productive hours measured separately from paid hours, with lineup and breaks excluded?
3. Is the standard calculated over seven consecutive days and recalculated against the actual room-status mix?
4. Does inspection cover 100% self-check, 15% to 25% supervisor check and 3% to 5% management audit?
5. Is the rework rate reported weekly and does it sit on the same dashboard as credits per hour?
6. Is room release lag measured in minutes and compared against the check-in guarantee?
7. Is overtime monitored by day of week and correlated with arrival peaks?
8. Has every change in amenity, linen program or brand triggered a formal revision of credits?
9. Is there a named person accountable for the standard, with authority to revise the schedule?
10. Are the three largest causes of rework this quarter named and attached to an action plan?
11. Has walking time to the linen room been measured in the last six months?
12. Has the standard been discussed with the floor team using the same numbers that appear in the report?

A standard audited across these twelve points stops being a spreadsheet and becomes a management system. That is the difference between knowing what it costs to clean a room and being able to prove it to an owner, a brand or an auditor.


## FAQ

**How many rooms should a room attendant clean per day?**
It depends on the tier. In economy and limited-service hotels, 15 to 17 rooms per 8-hour shift is a common range. In select service, 13 to 15. In upscale, 11 to 13. In luxury and resort, 8 to 11. The right number is the one that results from your actual mix of checkouts, stayovers and square footage.

**What is a room credit?**
It is the normalized amount of work a room represents. A standard checkout is worth 1,0; a light stayover about 0,5; a turndown about 0,2. Without that table, two people comparing productivity are counting different work.

**Do productive hours include breaks and lineup?**
No. Productive hours exclude meals, breaks, lineup, cart setup and trips to the linen room. Calculating the standard over paid hours inflates the result by 10% to 18%.

**What is the difference between a productivity standard and a brand standard?**
The brand standard defines the minimum service the flag requires. The productivity standard defines how much time that minimum consumes. The second has to be built from the first, never against it.

**How do you measure productivity without hurting quality?**
By putting inspection inside the standard. A room only counts as complete once it is released by inspection, and the rework rate sits on the weekly dashboard next to credits per hour.

**Is it worth outsourcing housekeeping?**
It is worth it when the problem is occupancy volatility, recruitment or weekend coverage — not when the problem is a wrong credit table. Outsourcing without a defined standard means buying a cheaper version of an undefined service.

**How often should the standard be reviewed?**
Quarterly, and whenever there is a change in the linen program, amenities, renovation or brand. Revising under cost pressure, with no operational change, is reducing service under another name.

**What is a healthy rework range?**
Below 8%. Above that, the apparent productivity standard is being propped up by hours that never appear in the calculation, and the real cost per room goes up.

**How many rooms per supervisor?**
A practical reference is 12 to 18 room attendants per floor supervisor, with dedicated coverage for inspection. Above that, inspection becomes symbolic sampling.

**How does this apply to boutique hotels?**
Boutique properties usually operate at the bottom of the tier: larger rooms, double bathrooms, differentiated amenities and turndown. The standard tends toward 1,5 to 2,2 credits per hour, and the variation between rooms is wider than in standardized inventories.
