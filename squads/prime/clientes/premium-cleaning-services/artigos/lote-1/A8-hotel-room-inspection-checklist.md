> **Direct answer:** A hotel room inspection checklist is a weighted list of conditions a released room must meet, scored so two inspectors reach the same conclusion. This 40-point version weights the bathroom at 30, bed and linen at 25, surfaces and floor at 25, amenities and technology at 12, and presentation and odor at 8, with any critical defect failing the room.

## Key takeaways

- Forty points is the practical ceiling: an inspection that takes more than eight minutes stops happening on a full house.
- Weight the score by guest impact, not by square footage. The bathroom carries 30% of the total because water, hair and mold produce the complaints that cost money.
- Score every point pass or fail. Partial credit is where inspectors drift and where a 94 stops meaning anything.
- One critical defect fails the room even at 98 points: blood, mold, pests, hair in the bed, urine odor, a broken lock or exposed wiring.
- A 90-point release threshold with 20% to 25% supervisor sampling catches more real defects than inspecting 100% of rooms badly.

## 1. Why does a hotel need a standardized room inspection checklist?

A laminated checklist is not an inspection system. Most hotels have one: fifty items in small type, hanging inside the housekeeping closet, initialed by whoever cleaned the room. It records that someone looked. It does not record what they saw, how serious it was, or whether the next inspector would have agreed.

The difference between a checklist and an inspection standard comes down to three properties. A shared definition of pass, so that "clean mirror" means the same thing on the third floor and the eleventh. A weight that reflects guest impact, so a streak on glass is not treated like a hair in the bed. And a score that can be compared across rooms, floors, shifts and months, so the conversation moves from adjectives to a trend line.

Without those three, every quality discussion becomes a negotiation. The executive housekeeper says the room was fine. The front office says the guest moved at 9 pm. The vendor says the defect was not in scope. Nobody has a document that settles it, so the most senior person in the room wins, which is rarely the person who actually inspected the room.

The cost of that ambiguity is not abstract. A room released at 3:40 pm with a hair in the bed is not a housekeeping defect at 3:40. It is a front-desk problem at 9 pm, when the property has no comparable room to move the guest into, and it becomes a refund, a loyalty gesture or a public review. The cleaning failure took ninety seconds to fix at 3:40 and several hundred dollars to fix at 9 pm.

A scored checklist also changes what a vendor can be asked to prove. "The room looked clean" is not a service level. "Ninety-two points, one major defect in the shower track, corrected in fourteen minutes, photographed before and after" is a service level, and it can sit inside a contract without argument. The operational sequence that precedes the inspection is covered in the [hotel turnover cleaning checklist](/hotel-turnover-cleaning-checklist); the inspection is what closes it.

## 2. What are the 40 points, and how are they grouped?

The 40 points divide into five zones, and the division follows how a guest experiences the room rather than how a cleaner moves through it. A guest enters, sees the bed and the floor, uses the bathroom, touches the amenities, and forms an opinion about smell within seconds. The weights follow that order of sensitivity.

Twelve points cover the bathroom, worth 30 of 100. Eight cover the bed and linen, worth 25. Nine cover surfaces and floor, worth 25. Six cover amenities and technology, worth 12. Five cover presentation and odor, worth 8.

The imbalance is deliberate. The bathroom is smaller than the bedroom in almost every hotel, yet it generates a disproportionate share of cleanliness complaints because water leaves evidence, grout holds it, and guests inspect it closely and privately. The bed carries the highest emotional weight because it is the surface a guest touches with their face. Surfaces and floor carry the most visible area, so they earn the same weight as the bed. Amenities and technology are weighted lower because there are fewer of them, but a dead remote converts a clean room into a broken room in one second.

Why forty and not a hundred? Because inspection competes with production for the same supervisor hours. A forty-point inspection takes six to nine minutes in a standard room, which means a supervisor can cover twenty-five to thirty-five rooms in a shift and still run the floor. A hundred-point inspection takes twenty minutes, so it gets sampled down to five rooms, and the property ends up with a beautiful instrument nobody uses.

Two rules keep the count honest. Every point must be visible from a standing position without moving furniture, except the four points that explicitly require bending or lifting. And every point must have a single pass criterion that fits in one sentence, because a criterion that needs a paragraph is a training document, not an inspection line.

## 3. What do the 12 bathroom points cover?

The bathroom is inspected top to bottom, dry to wet, back to front, with the light at a grazing angle. Overhead light flattens water spots and film; a light held low and across the surface reveals them. This is the single technique that separates an inspector who finds defects from one who signs the sheet.

Start with the toilet, which is the point most often scored from the wrong angle. The bowl, the rim underside, the hinge caps and the base are separate checks because soil hides under the rim and behind the hinges while the visible bowl looks acceptable. Then the shower and tub: interior, drain, overflow, glass, door tracks and the surround. The track is where the score is usually lost, because it collects hair and soap residue that a quick wipe pushes to the corners.

The sink area follows: basin, overflow hole, faucet, aerator, handles, countertop, backsplash and mirror. The overflow hole and the aerator are the two places where a room can look spotless and still fail a brand audit. The mirror is checked at an angle for streaks and at the edges for dust, because edges are what a guest sees when they lean in.

Finish with the hardware and the envelope: towel bars, rings, tissue holder, floor corners, behind the door, exhaust vent, ceiling and walls. The vent grille is a dust tell. If it is clogged, the room has an air quality problem that will read as an odor problem later.

| # | Bathroom point | Pass standard | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Toilet bowl, rim underside, hinges | No soil, no stain, no odor at the water line; hinges clean under the caps | 4 |
| 2 | Toilet base and floor behind | No splash, hair or dust at the base or behind the bowl | 3 |
| 3 | Shower and tub interior, drain | No soap film, no hair in the drain, no standing water, no rust | 4 |
| 4 | Shower glass, door and tracks | Glass clear at a low angle; tracks free of hair, grit and residue | 3 |
| 5 | Tub surround and grout | Grout uniform in color; no mold, mildew or missing caulk | 2 |
| 6 | Sink basin and overflow | Basin dry and clear; overflow hole free of residue | 3 |
| 7 | Faucet, handles and aerator | No water spots, no lime build-up, aerator clear, handles aligned | 2 |
| 8 | Countertop and backsplash | Dry, no rings, no hair, no residue at the wall joint | 2 |
| 9 | Mirror and edges | No streaks at a low angle; edges and corners dust-free | 2 |
| 10 | Towel bars, rings, tissue holder | No dust on top surfaces, no fingerprints, hardware tight | 2 |
| 11 | Floor, corners and behind door | No hair, no grit in corners, no splash behind the door | 2 |
| 12 | Exhaust vent, ceiling and walls | Grille dust-free, no splatter on walls, no ceiling stains | 1 |

The weights matter less than the binary decision inside each line. A mirror is either streak-free at a low angle or it is not. An inspector who starts writing "minor streaks" has begun to negotiate with the standard, and the score stops being comparable across inspectors.

## 4. What do the 8 bed and linen points cover?

The bed is the only part of the room a guest touches with their face, which is why eight points and a quarter of the score sit here. It is also the zone where the cheapest defect causes the most expensive reaction: a single hair on a pillowcase can trigger a room move even when everything else is perfect.

Inspect the bed from the foot, then walk one side, then the other. The mattress pad is checked for alignment and stains, because a pad that has slipped leaves the mattress protector exposed and signals that the bed was made in a hurry. The fitted sheet must be tight enough that a hand swept across it does not gather fabric, and it must be the correct size, since an undersized sheet pops at the corners by midnight.

The duvet or cover is checked for centering, creasing and cleanliness at the fold, which is where guests look first. Pillowcases are checked for count, seam orientation, stains and hair, and the count has to match the brand standard rather than the housekeeping closet's habit. Four pillows with two cases is a defect even if the bed looks symmetrical.

Then the structure: bed skirt or base, headboard, and the space under the bed. Under-bed is a five-second check with a flashlight and it is where dust bunnies, previous guests' belongings and missed trash accumulate. The headboard is checked for dust on the top edge and for marks at hand height.

The last point is presentation: the fold, the accent, the alignment with the nightstands. This is the point that separates a room that is clean from a room that looks cared for, and it is the one most often cut when the floor is running late.

| # | Bed and linen point | Pass standard | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Mattress pad | Correctly aligned, no stains, no wrinkles under the sheet | 3 |
| 2 | Fitted sheet | Tight at all four corners, correct size, no crease across the sleeping area | 4 |
| 3 | Duvet or cover | Centered, no crease at the fold, no stain or hair on the exposed surface | 4 |
| 4 | Pillowcases | Brand count, seams hidden, no stains, no hair, no wrinkles | 4 |
| 5 | Bed skirt or base | Straight, no dust line, no tear, no gap at the floor | 2 |
| 6 | Under-bed and headboard | No dust, trash or belongings under the bed; headboard clean at hand height | 3 |
| 7 | Linen count versus standard | Matches the brand or contract count exactly, including pillow and towel sets | 3 |
| 8 | Presentation and symmetry | Bed aligned with the nightstands, fold consistent, accent placed as specified | 2 |

Linen count is the point that most often exposes a vendor problem rather than an attendant problem. If the count drifts down by one pillowcase across a floor, the cause is usually par stock or laundry supply, not carelessness, and the inspection should code it that way so the fix lands in the right place.

## 5. What do the 9 surface and floor points cover?

Surfaces are inspected at three heights, because dust settles differently at each. Below knee level, look for grit, hair and splash. At seated eye level, look across the surface at a low angle for dust film and streaks. Above shoulder level, look up for cobwebs, vent dust and ceiling marks. Most inspectors only check the middle band, which is why high dust and floor edges are the two most common repeat defects in a property that believes it is clean.

The nine points run through the room in a fixed order so that nothing is skipped when the floor is running late. Nightstands first, including the top, the underside of the top, the drawer interiors and the back edge against the wall. Then the desk and chair: top, legs, the back of the chair, and the area under the desk where feet rest. Then the TV console and the screen, which is checked with the TV off and a light at an angle.

Lamps and switches follow: shades dust-free and correctly oriented, bulbs working, switches and outlets free of fingerprints. Window treatments are checked for even pleating, dust on the sill and marks on the glass. The closet is checked for hangers, the safe, the iron and ironing board, and dust on the shelf. Floor coverings come next, with attention to carpet edges, the perimeter under furniture and any visible stain. Hard floors and grout are checked for film, which is the residue that makes tile look dull even after mopping. Walls, baseboards, doors and frames close the sequence.

1. Nightstands, tops, undersides and drawers.
2. Desk, chair and the space beneath the desk.
3. TV console, screen and the shelf behind it.
4. Lamps, shades, bulbs, switches and outlets.
5. Window sill, glass and curtain pleating.
6. Closet, hangers, safe, iron and shelf.
7. Carpet edges, perimeter and stains under furniture.
8. Hard floor and grout film, where applicable.
9. Walls, baseboards, door and frame.

Carpet is the point where a visual check stops being enough. A carpet that looks acceptable under overhead light can show traffic lanes and spotting at a low angle, and a room with a visible stain should not be released to inventory regardless of the rest of the score. Properties with heavy turnover often need a separate extraction program between deep cleans, which is a different service from daily vacuuming and is priced differently in any [high-traffic carpet cleaning](/commercial-carpet-cleaning-high-traffic) scope.

## 6. What do the 6 amenity and technology points cover?

Amenities and technology are weighted at 12 points because the failures here are binary. A coffee setup is either complete or it is not. A remote either works or the guest calls the front desk. There is no partial credit worth arguing about, and that makes this zone the fastest to inspect and the easiest to audit remotely through guest complaints.

The coffee and tea setup is checked for the brand's exact configuration: machine, pods or packets, cups, lids, stirrers, sugar and creamer, all present and sealed. The refrigerator or minibar is checked for temperature, stock, seals and odors; a refrigerator above 40 degrees Fahrenheit is a food safety issue, not a housekeeping preference. The ice bucket and glasses are checked for cleanliness, correct placement and a fresh liner.

Technology starts with the remote: batteries, pairing, channel list and the correct input. A remote that cannot reach the streaming input will generate a call even if every channel works. The TV itself is checked for a clean screen, correct welcome channel and volume level. The thermostat is checked against the property's set point, because a room set to 60 degrees in summer reads as a maintenance failure. The Wi-Fi card or QR code must be present and current, and every outlet and USB port in the room should be tested at least once per quarter rather than once per stay.

1. Coffee and tea setup, complete and sealed.
2. Refrigerator or minibar, temperature, stock and seals.
3. Ice bucket, glasses and liner.
4. Remote, batteries, input and channel list.
5. Thermostat set point and Wi-Fi information.
6. Outlets, USB ports and lamp switches.

A dead outlet is the defect guests report last and remember longest, because it surfaces when they try to charge a phone at midnight. Testing all six points takes under a minute and eliminates an entire category of complaint.

## 7. What do the 5 presentation and odor points cover?

Presentation is the summary judgment a guest makes in the first three seconds, before they inspect anything. It is worth 8 points because it is cheap to fix and expensive to ignore. Odor is included here because smell is the only defect that cannot be photographed, is rarely logged, and is the first thing a guest mentions in a review.

The first point is alignment: bed centered between nightstands, furniture square to the wall, chair tucked at a consistent angle, lamps symmetrical. The second is curtains and lighting: pleats even, sheer closed to the same width in every room, lamps on the brand's arrival scene. The third is trash: bins empty, liners hidden, no evidence of the previous guest in the bathroom or the desk.

The fourth is odor, and it deserves its own protocol. The inspector stands in the doorway, closes the door for ten seconds, then opens it and breathes normally. The room should smell of nothing. A room that smells of cleaning chemical is masking a source, and the source is usually the drain trap, the AC coil, the carpet pad, the linen or the trash chute. Perfume and aerosol sprays are not a fix; they are a delay.

The fifth point is the door-frame test: stand in the doorway with the room lit as a guest would find it and scan left to right for anything that breaks the composition. A crooked lamp shade, a visible cable, a towel edge out of line. This is the point that catches what the other thirty-nine missed, and it takes five seconds.

## 8. How do you score a room on a 100-point scale?

Scoring is the part most programs get wrong, because they mix weights, severity and partial credit into a single number that nobody can reproduce. The model below avoids that by keeping three things separate: the weight of each point, the pass or fail result of each point, and the severity of any defect found.

Each point is worth its weight and is scored as earned or not earned. There is no 0.5. The room score is the sum of earned weights, so a perfect room scores 100 and a room that misses the mirror and the shower track scores 95. Severity is recorded separately as critical, major or minor, and it drives the action rather than the number.

| Score band | Rating | Action | Who re-inspects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 95 – 100 | Release ready | Release to inventory; log as clean | No re-inspection |
| 90 – 94 | Release with note | Release after correcting listed defects; note in log | Attendant self-check with photo |
| 80 – 89 | Rework | Correct within 20 minutes before release | Floor supervisor |
| Below 80 | Re-clean | Full re-clean; root cause coded; supervisor review | Executive housekeeper |
| Any score with a critical defect | Fail | Do not release; correct and re-inspect from the top | Executive housekeeper |

The critical list is short and should not be debated. Blood or bodily fluid, mold or mildew visible in the bathroom, any pest evidence, hair in the bed, urine or smoke odor, a lock or latch that does not secure, exposed wiring, and standing water or an active leak. Any one of these fails the room regardless of the other thirty-nine points. A room at 98 with a hair in the bed is a failed room, and treating it as a 98 is how a property trains its inspectors to miss the only defects that matter.

Two supporting rules make the model survive contact with a real floor. First, the score is computed by the inspector at the door, not reconstructed later from memory. Second, the defect list is written in the same order as the checklist so that a trend report can be built without re-reading free text.

## 9. What score separates pass from fail, and what happens on a fail?

A release threshold of 90 is the common operating choice in US hotels, paired with zero critical defects. The threshold is not sacred. A luxury property may run 95, a limited-service property may run 88, and a property in the middle of a renovation may run a temporary 85. What matters is that the threshold is written down, applied to every room, and not adjusted on a Friday afternoon because the floor is behind.

When a room fails, the response has four steps and a clock. Tag the room as not ready in the property management system. Correct the defect within twenty minutes for a major item, or re-clean for a score below 80. Re-inspect by a supervisor rather than the attendant who cleaned it, because self-verification after a failure is not verification. Log the defect with a photo, the point number and a root cause code.

The root cause code is what turns an inspection program into a management system. Most programs stop at "shower track dirty" and then retrain the whole team on bathrooms. A coded defect usually points somewhere else: no squeegee on the cart, a track brush that has worn out, a 14-minute room allocation that does not allow for a proper track clean, or a supervisor who is inspecting from the doorway.

| Defect | Usual root cause | Fix that holds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shower track residue | No track brush on the cart; room credit too tight | Add the brush and 90 seconds to the room credit |
| Hair on a pillowcase | Linen checked at folding, not at the bed | Check at the bed and keep a lint roller on the cart |
| High dust on vent or headboard | Inspector only scans the middle band | Add a shoulder-height and above pass to self-check |
| Debris under the bed | Vacuum head does not reach the center | Long wand plus a five-second flashlight check |
| Streaks at mirror edges | Glass wiped with a dry cloth | Two-cloth method with a dedicated edge pass |
| Incomplete coffee setup | Restocked from memory at the end of shift | Photo standard at par level, checked on the tray |
| Odor at the doorway | Dry drain trap or soiled AC coil | Prime traps weekly and put the coil on a schedule |

The economics are easy to underestimate. A rework consumes fifteen to twenty-five minutes including the walk, the correction and the second inspection. At 120 rooms per day and a 10% rework rate, that is twelve rooms and roughly four hours of labor per day, or half a full-time position, before counting the room move or the refund that follows a missed defect. That number belongs in the same report as the score, because it is the number an owner will act on.

## 10. Who should inspect, how often, and how many rooms?

Inspection coverage is a sampling problem, not a coverage problem. Inspecting every room is only possible if the inspection is so fast that it is meaningless. The practical model layers four levels of coverage with different purposes, and each level answers a different question.

| Role | Coverage | Frequency | Question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Room attendant | 100% of assigned rooms | Every room, every shift | Did I complete every point on my own room? |
| Floor supervisor | 20% – 25% of released rooms | Every shift, spread across floors | Is the floor releasing rooms that meet the standard? |
| Executive housekeeper | 5% – 10% of rooms | Daily, rotating by floor and room type | Is the standard itself holding across the property? |
| Rooms director or GM | 2 – 3 rooms | Weekly | Does the guest experience match the report? |
| Brand or third-party auditor | 10 – 20 rooms | Quarterly | Does the property pass an external standard? |

The sampling has to be random and it has to include the risky inventory. Supervisors should not inspect the rooms of the attendants they are training, and they should not accept a room chosen by the attendant. The sample must include arrivals, because a checkout is inspected under time pressure and an arrival is inspected after the room has sat, and it must include at least one suite or accessible room per day, because those room types carry extra points that standard rooms do not.

The math is manageable. A 150-room property with 25% supervisor coverage inspects roughly 38 rooms per day. At seven minutes per room that is about 4.4 hours of supervisor time, which is one supervisor's inspection block and not an additional headcount. A property that cannot afford that block is not saving money; it is moving the cost to the front desk.

## 11. How do you keep inspectors calibrated?

Two inspectors can look at the same room and score it twelve points apart without either of them being dishonest. One inspects from the doorway, the other from inside the room. One counts a faint water spot as a fail, the other as acceptable. Left uncorrected, that drift makes every trend line meaningless and every vendor dispute unwinnable.

Calibration is a monthly routine, not a training event. Pick five rooms with known scores, including one borderline room and one room with a single critical defect. Have every inspector score them independently, then compare point by point. A spread wider than five points on the same room means the pass criteria need rewriting, not that the inspector needs discipline.

Three practices reduce drift. A photo library of pass and fail examples for every point, taken in the property's own rooms, so the standard is visual rather than verbal. Paired inspections, where a new inspector and an experienced one walk the same room and compare notes before the score is entered. And a quarterly audit of the auditor, where the executive housekeeper re-scores a sample of supervisor inspections and reports the variance.

The structural risk is role conflict. When the person who inspects also has to release rooms to hit a 3 pm check-in guarantee, the inspection bends. Separating quality assurance from production, even by one shift or one person, is worth more than any amount of retraining.

## 12. How do you turn inspection scores into training?

An inspection program that produces a score but no training loop becomes a scoreboard, and scoreboards change behavior for a quarter and then stop. The loop that works is narrow and repetitive: find the three most frequent defects, teach those three, verify the change in the next inspection cycle.

Start with a Pareto of defects by point number over thirty days. In most properties, three to five points account for more than half of all deductions. The pattern is usually consistent: shower track, under-bed, high dust on the vent or headboard, mirror edges, and the coffee setup. Those five become the training agenda for the month, not the forty.

Then run short drills rather than long sessions. A ten-minute huddle before the shift, with a before-and-after photo of the actual defect on the actual floor, changes more behavior than a ninety-minute classroom session. The attendant sees the standard applied to a room they recognize, which removes the "that is not what they mean" escape.

Track the defect rate by point, by room type and by shift, and review it every two weeks. If the shower track defect rate falls after the drill and the under-bed rate rises, the training worked and the attention moved. That is normal, and it is why the agenda rotates.

Keep the individual data private. The purpose of coding defects by attendant is to target coaching, not to publish a ranking. A property that posts names on a wall gets compliance for two weeks and then gets inspectors who score generously to protect colleagues, which destroys the data.

## 13. How do you report scores without turning them into a weapon?

The weekly report should fit on one page and answer four questions: what is the score, is it moving, what are the top defects, and what is being done about them. Anything longer is not read, and anything that leads with a ranking is defended rather than used.

The page should carry the average score, the percentage of rooms above 90, the rework rate, the release lag and the top five defect points with counts. A trend line over eight weeks matters more than any single week, because a single week is noise and a trend is a management signal. The property should also report the sample size, since an average built on six rooms is not comparable to one built on thirty-eight.

For a vendor relationship, the same page becomes the monthly service report. It should show the vendor's rooms separately from any in-house rooms, with the same weights and the same sampling method, so the comparison is fair. If the vendor inspects its own rooms, the report should say so, and the client should retain an independent sample.

The tone matters commercially. A report that says "the team is failing" produces arguments about the sample. A report that says "shower tracks account for 31% of deductions this month, the cart brush was replaced on the 12th, and the rate fell from 22% to 9%" produces a conversation about the next constraint. The second report is the one that survives a quarterly business review.

## 14. How do you write inspection standards into a vendor contract?

An inspection standard that lives only in an operations manual is not enforceable. It becomes enforceable when four things are attached to the contract: the forty points with their pass criteria and weights, the critical defect list, the sampling method and the reporting deadline.

The scope clause should state that the vendor inspects and scores every released room against the attached standard, that the client may audit any room at any time, and that the vendor must provide the raw inspection data rather than a summary. The quality clause should set the release threshold, the maximum acceptable rework rate and the cure period for a sustained miss, typically two consecutive months.

Payment should be tied to the score in a way that is simple enough to administer. A common structure holds a small percentage of the monthly invoice against a score floor and a rework ceiling, released when the monthly report is delivered and the independent sample confirms the result. Complex penalty matrices tend to be negotiated away in the first dispute.

Three clauses prevent the most common failures. A clause requiring the vendor to participate in joint calibration, so both parties score the same rooms and compare. A clause requiring rework at the vendor's cost when a failed room is found by the client's audit. And a clause granting the client access to the defect log, because a vendor that will not share the log is usually managing the report rather than the rooms.

The commercial logic is the same one that governs any service contract: you cannot price what you cannot define. The [cost and pricing guide for hotel housekeeping](/hotel-housekeeping-cost-pricing-guide-2026) shows how the same logic applies to labor and credits, and the [hidden costs of inconsistent cleaning](/insights/hidden-costs-inconsistent-commercial-cleaning-nj-ny) shows what an undefined standard costs once it reaches the guest.

## 15. How do you launch the program in 30 days?

Week one is construction. Write the forty points in the property's language, define the pass criterion for each in one sentence, assign weights, and photograph ten rooms that represent pass and fail for every point. The photo library is the deliverable that makes the rest work, and it takes longer than the writing.

Week two is baseline. Inspect thirty rooms with two inspectors working independently, score them, and compare. Do not publish targets this week. The purpose is to find out how far apart the inspectors are and which points are ambiguous, because those are the points that will be argued about for the next year.

Week three is training and calibration. Run the ten-minute huddles on the top five defects found in the baseline, rewrite any point that produced a spread wider than five points, and certify the inspectors on the five calibration rooms. Publish the standard, the weights and the critical list in the same meeting for attendants, supervisors and the front office.

Week four is live operation. Release rooms against the 90-point threshold, log every defect, review the daily report for fifteen minutes, and publish the first weekly page. Expect the score to dip for the first ten days as the team adjusts to being inspected, and resist the urge to lower the threshold during the dip.

The failure mode is compressing the four weeks into one and calling the result a standard. A score produced without a baseline is a target, and a target without calibration is an opinion with a decimal point.

## FAQ

**What should be on a hotel room inspection checklist?**
The conditions a released room must meet, grouped by zone and weighted by guest impact. This version uses 40 points: 12 in the bathroom, 8 on the bed and linen, 9 on surfaces and floor, 6 on amenities and technology, and 5 on presentation and odor.

**What is a passing score for a hotel room inspection?**
Ninety out of 100 is the common US operating threshold, paired with zero critical defects. Luxury properties often set 95, limited-service properties 88, and the number matters less than applying it consistently to every room.

**How many rooms should a supervisor inspect per day?**
Twenty to twenty-five percent of released rooms, spread across floors and room types. On a 150-room property that is roughly 38 rooms, or about 4.4 hours at seven minutes per room.

**What counts as a critical defect in a hotel room?**
Blood or bodily fluid, visible mold, pest evidence, hair in the bed, urine or smoke odor, a lock that does not secure, exposed wiring, and standing water or an active leak. Any one of these fails the room regardless of the total score.

**How long should a room inspection take?**
Six to nine minutes for a standard room using 40 points. A suite or accessible room takes ten to twelve minutes because of the extra bathroom, living area and fixture checks.

**Should housekeeping inspect every room?**
Attendants self-check every room in under a minute, but supervisor inspection is sampled at 20% to 25%. Inspecting 100% of rooms usually means the inspection is too shallow to catch anything.

**What is the difference between a stayover inspection and a checkout inspection?**
A checkout is scored on all 40 points after a full clean. A stayover uses a reduced set of about 22 points covering the bed, bathroom touch-up, trash, amenities and floor, and it should be scored on its own scale rather than compared to checkout scores.

**How do you inspect a room for odor?**
Stand in the doorway, close the door for ten seconds, reopen it and breathe normally. The room should smell of nothing. A chemical or perfume smell indicates masking, and the source is usually the drain, the AC coil, the carpet or the linen.

**How often should the checklist be updated?**
Annually, and whenever the brand standard, the amenity program, the linen program or the room inventory changes. Every change requires new photos and a calibration session before the revised points go live.

**Can a 40-point checklist replace a brand quality assurance audit?**
No. It is an operating tool that keeps daily release quality consistent. Brand audits sample against the flag's own standard and documentation format, and the checklist should be built to feed those audits rather than substitute for them.
