> **Direct answer:** A commercial cleaning RFP is the procurement document that turns a cleaning scope into an auditable, comparable offer. Twelve mandatory clauses protect the facility manager: measurable scope, SLA targets and penalties, insurance and bonding, personnel and training, product specifications, and pricing with escalation and reinstatement terms.

## Key takeaways

- Twelve clauses are mandatory in a commercial cleaning RFP, and the measurable scope clause is the root from which every SLA, penalty, insurance and price flows.
- A cleaning scope written in condition and check-interval language produces comparable bids, while a labor-only scope produces a dozen different products at a dozen different prices.
- Insurance and bonding minimums belong in the RFP with numbers, not adjectives, and the indemnity layer should be mutual.
- Penalties and liquidated damages should be capped, scheduled and tied to specific missed targets, not to subjective appearance judgments.
- Pricing should be rebuilt from the scope with an escalation index and a reinstatement clause, or the RFP cannot support a fair comparison.

## 1. What is a commercial cleaning RFP, and why do facility managers need mandatory clauses?

A commercial cleaning RFP is the procurement document that turns a cleaning scope into an auditable, comparable offer. It names the spaces, the tasks, the frequencies, the standards and the reporting expectations, and it asks vendors to respond against those terms. The facility manager who publishes one is not changing the logic of the buying process. The buyer is making the cleaning need explicit, and explicit is comparable.

Most cleaning buying decisions never travel through a document. Facilities teams that keep an internal crew, or that have operated without ever writing a scope for an outsourced partner, tend to issue requests for proposal only when an incumbent leaves, when the footprint changes, or when an invoice arrives with a surprise. The negotiation that follows is run against memory, and memory is the least reliable asset a facilities manager owns.

The consequence is predictable. The first proposal gets compared with the second as if both arrived from the same universe, and they usually do not. Labor availability becomes the sorting key, and the narrower vendor wins. The buyer then pays for the narrowest version of a service it never specified, and the gap appears months later as a renegotiation the client did not plan for.

A commercial cleaning RFP is not a preference list, and the distinction matters. A preference list produces opinions. An RFP produces clauses, and clauses produce deliverables.

Twelve mandatory clauses form the working spine of a serious commercial cleaning RFP. Each one removes a specific failure mode that otherwise migrates from the operation into the contract, and each one converts an argument from a negotiation into a deliverable. The clauses run from the measurable scope through the service-level agreement, the penalty structure, the insurance and bonding block, the personnel and training requirements, the product and equipment specifications, and the pricing, escalation and reinstatement terms.

The reason the facility manager needs the document is not procurement theater. It is the arithmetic of the buying process. Without a written scope, vendors price labor. Without a written SLA, vendors price absence. Without insurance minimums, the client prices the risk onto a guess. Without penalty clauses, the client prices the recovery onto the vendor. Every one of those substitutions moves the comparison away from the delivered service and toward the negotiating posture, and that is where facilities budgets quietly stop being controllable.

Voluntary frameworks are the norm in most commercial cleaning requests because they are easier to publish and easier to respond to, and easier to ignore. The mandatory version is a procurement posture rather than a preference posture, and the difference shows up in the quality of the proposals that come back. A facility manager that publishes twelve mandatory clauses is telling the market that the scope, the agreement, the penalty, the insurance, the personnel, the products and the pricing will not be negotiated around, and that posture is what produces proposals that can be compared line by line instead of compared by instinct.

For procurement teams in New Jersey and New York, the mandatory clause set is usually published alongside a request for quotation template, and the template is what produces the download that the program counts. The buyer that moves from a voluntary questionnaire to a mandatory clause set is buying a different category of conversation, because the first produces opinions and the second produces evidence.

The buyer that uses the mandatory set end to end is buying a procurement system, not a cleaning service. The system includes the scope, the agreement, the penalty, the insurance, the personnel, the products, the pricing, the renewal, the transition and the evidence; the cleaning service is only the delivery of the first item, and the facility manager that confuses the two will read the contract before it reads the scope, and will negotiate before it will measure.

## 2. How do you write a measurable cleaning scope for a commercial cleaning RFP?

The single most important clause in any commercial cleaning RFP is the scope, and the single most common error in that clause is describing labor instead of condition.

Daily housekeeping means nothing to a vendor that cannot price it and nothing to a client that cannot audit it. The scope has to be written in operational language, in zones, with condition standards, check intervals and recovery times.

A measurable scope runs on three clocks, the same clocks that govern a public area standard. The first clock is condition, written as a pass or fail standard at a stated distance and light level. The second clock is check interval, the interval at which a trained person verifies condition between services. The third clock is recovery time, the window in which a reported defect returns the zone to standard.

From those three clocks the scope derives task frequencies. A restroom that must be recovered within fifteen minutes and checked every forty-five minutes gets a different schedule than a restroom checked once daily. A lobby floor that must be dry within a stated window gets a different mop routine. The frequency follows the standard, and the standard follows the clocks.

The result is a scope that can be priced, scheduled and audited. It can be handed to a vendor and receive a proposal. It can be handed to a supervisor and receive a shift plan. It can be handed to an inspector and receive a score.

For an office or medical office account, the scope should also translate into desk counts or square footage, because those two inputs are what a vendor can price without inventing capacity. A medical office adds the clinical disinfection layer, and that layer changes both the frequency and the product requirement, which means the scope and the SLA have to move together rather than independently.

The scope fence matters commercially because the first vendor quote that falls outside the fence is usually a quote for a narrower service wearing a broader name, and the facility manager that accepts it has already lost the comparison before the numbers are read. A written fence turns that risk into a named exclusion, and a named exclusion is a clause rather than a hope.

A written scope also protects the vendor from absorbing costs that were never priced, which is the commercial reason facilities managers resist writing scope in condition language in the first place; the resistance is usually about the difficulty of describing the work, and the difficulty is usually about the absence of a zone table rather than about the work itself.

## 3. What should a commercial cleaning RFP scope include across zones and support areas?

A complete commercial cleaning scope covers the spaces the cleaning team actually serves, and it separates them by function rather than by a single blanket line.

Guest room programs cover checkouts, stayovers, turndowns and deep cleans, usually expressed in room credit logic. Open office programs cover desks, common areas, restrooms and kitchenettes. Medical office programs add clinical disinfection requirements that a general commercial scope does not address. Support areas cover back-of-house corridors, locker rooms, laundry and waste handling.

The scope should also name the surfaces that carry the risk. Restrooms are a zone of their own, because supply, hardware and odor failures are mostly supply and hardware failures rather than soil failures. Commercial kitchens carry degreasing requirements that general purpose cleaner cannot meet, and the [commercial cleaning services in New Jersey](/commercial-cleaning-services-nj) page maps the program structure that fits each of these zones.

Support areas are where most cleaning programs leak scope without noticing it, because the back-of-house corridor, the locker room and the waste handling rarely appear on the original scope and then appear as a change order after the first invoice.

The zone list is what makes the RFP producible as a download, and a document that cannot be turned into a checklist or a table of zones does not become the reference the buyer intends, and the buyer intends it to become the document that generates the next three requests for proposal.

Zones with clinical or food-contact surfaces need the condition standard written at the surface level, because a general surface condition standard will not survive the inspection of a health regulator or a brand auditor.

The zone level condition standard is what makes the scope auditable, and the auditability is what makes the comparison possible, and the comparison is what makes the RFP worth publishing.

## 4. What SLA metrics and response targets should a commercial cleaning RFP require?

The service-level agreement converts the scope into a score. Without an SLA, the cleaning program has no definition of done, and the weekly report becomes a narrative rather than a measurement.

A serious cleaning SLA names metrics, targets and consequences. The metrics usually required in a commercial cleaning RFP are condition score, check compliance, complaint response, deep clean adherence and response time.

| KPI | Typical required target | Reporting cadence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Condition score | 4.5 / 5 or equivalent pass threshold | Weekly |
| Restroom check compliance | 95% or higher of scheduled checks | Daily log |
| Guest complaint response | Same shift or fifteen-minute recovery | Log with timestamps |
| Deep clean adherence | 100% of scheduled deep cleans | Monthly |

The SLA block should also carry the consequences that make the targets enforceable, and those consequences are the penalty clause covered next.

The SLA block should also name the metric owner, because a target without an owner is a target that will be renegotiated rather than met, and a cleaning program without a named metric owner will drift back into opinion within one quarter.

The consequence structure should be written before the metric list, because a consequence that is defined only after a miss occurs is a consequence that will be invented at the moment of the miss.

An SLA that names a metric without a consequence produces a report rather than a control, and the cleaning program that reports rather than controls will not improve, which is why the consequence has to be written before the metric.

Penalties should be expressed as a percentage of the monthly service value or as a per-violation amount, and the facility manager should publish the cap before the first proposal is issued, because a cap that appears after a dispute is a cap that will be negotiated rather than applied.

## 5. How should a commercial cleaning RFP define penalties and liquidated damages?

The penalty clause is where a commercial cleaning RFP protects the client without converting the client into the collection agent.

Liquidated damages should be capped, scheduled and tied to specific missed targets. A clause that triggers on subjective judgment, on the phrase that a room looks clean, produces disputes that no schedule can resolve.

A workable penalty structure names the target, the measurement, the amount and the cure period, and it separates recoverable service failures from compensable performance failures.

Penalty caps should be stated as a percentage of the monthly service value or as a per-violation amount, and the cure period should be short enough to preserve the service relationship while long enough to be credible. A cure period that is shorter than the reporting cycle is a penalty that will never be applied, and a penalty that is uncapped will be renegotiated before it is ever used.

## 6. What insurance, bonding and indemnity clauses should a commercial cleaning RFP include?

The insurance block is the risk transfer layer, and it belongs in the RFP with numbers rather than adjectives.

| Coverage | Typical required minimum in an RFP | What it protects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial general liability | $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate | Bodily injury and property damage from cleaning operations |
| Workers compensation | Statutory basis | Employee injury, administered under the employer state |
| Surety bond | Case by case, commonly $50k to $250k for commercial cleaning | Subcontractor performance, subcontractor indemnity |
| Commercial auto | $1M combined single limit | Vendor vehicles used on client sites |

The indemnity clause should be mutual for the cleaning scope, because both parties carry risk the other cannot fully control. A one-way indemnity in a commercial cleaning RFP is a clause that protects the vendor's balance sheet while exporting the client's exposure.

The indemnification should also name the scope it covers, because a cleaning contract that indemnifies without naming the scope will be read as covering the whole relationship once a dispute starts.

The insurance block should also state the certificate holder, because a certificate naming the wrong party is a certificate that will not be honored when it matters.

The bond and the indemnity together answer the two questions that a facility manager asks informally and cannot afford to ask informally: who pays if a sub performs badly, and who pays if the indemnifying party is itself exposed; the written form of both is the only version that survives a dispute.

The insurance block should also state the policy limits in writing and attach them to the indemnification, because a limit that lives in a binder and an indemnification that lives in a clause are two documents that will not hold together in a dispute.

## 7. What personnel and training requirements should a commercial cleaning RFP state?

The personnel block in a commercial cleaning RFP converts hiring risk into a deliverable.

Background checks, training hours, supervision ratio and a named site supervisor are the usual requirements. Training should be scoped to the scope, because a scope that includes degreasing needs a training track that covers degreasing, and a scope that includes medical disinfection needs a track that covers it.

The supervision ratio should be stated as a number of attendants per supervisor, with the inspection layer attached. A ratio without an inspection layer is a staffing number, not a quality number. The [janitorial services in New Jersey](/janitorial-services-nj) page sets out the service model used for these accounts.

Training should be recorded in hours by role, because a general training statement cannot be audited and cannot be compared across proposals.

## 8. What product and equipment specifications should a commercial cleaning RFP require?

Product and equipment specifications protect the client from safety and damage exposure, and they are usually the fastest way to reduce cleaning cost without reducing cleaning.

An RFP should require an approved chemical list, dilution control, microfiber program, equipment availability, and consumable par levels. The specifications should be written by the surfaces being cleaned rather than by a single general list.

The practical effect is a cleaner invoice, because every product line is justified by a specification the client wrote before the vendor priced it.

Equipment specifications should also address consumables at specification, because a cleaning program that specifies a chemical without specifying a par level will fail on the first stockout cycle.

## 9. How should a commercial cleaning RFP handle pricing, escalation and change orders?

Pricing should be rebuilt from the scope, not negotiated from the first vendor's number.

An RFP should state the base scope price, the escalation mechanism, the change order process and the cap on each. Pricing that cannot be expressed in those terms cannot be compared, and a proposal that cannot be compared should not be accepted.

Escalation should be index based rather than purely inflationary, because a cleaning contract lives longer than the wage quote behind it. Change orders should describe the scope delta, the price delta and the approval path, so that a mid-contract change is not absorbed as goodwill.

Pricing should also state what is excluded from the base scope and therefore billable as a change order, because the exclusion list is what protects the client from the classic failure of paying for a deep clean inside the monthly service.

Escalation should be capped and the cap should be stated, because an uncapped escalation clause becomes a negotiation the client will lose.

## 10. What renewal, termination and service-gap clauses should a commercial cleaning RFP require?

A cleaning RFP should end with its commercial terms, and the service-gap clause is the one that converts the commercial terms into an operational promise.

Renewal should state the notice period, the price adjustment basis and the renewal options. Termination should state the cure period, the notice window and the access obligations that make a transition possible. The reinstatement clause should define what happens when a scheduled service does not start on the planned date, because the cost of a silent gap is larger than any fee line in the document.

The transition clause should also define the evidence handover, because the client that loses the inspection records after a transition loses the ability to defend the standard in any later dispute.

The termination clause should also require the return of equipment and the handling of bonded personnel records, because those are the operational details that become disputes.

## 11. How should a facility manager compare commercial cleaning proposals fairly in an RFP?

Comparisons should happen at the unit of the scope, not at the unit of the vendor's convenience.

The normalization approach is to convert every proposal into the same unit before comparing. Scope hours, condition standards, check intervals, recovery times and reporting requirements should all be equal inputs. Then the price comparison is meaningful, and the vendor comparison is a comparison of delivery rather than of positioning.

The [office cleaning services in New Jersey](/office-cleaning-new-jersey) page and the [commercial cleaning cost pricing guide for New Jersey](/insights/commercial-cleaning-cost-nj-pricing-guide-2026) both describe the normalization that makes two bids comparable.

A fair comparison also requires the vendor to answer the same questions in the same order, and the RFP should require that the response set covers scope, timeline, price, insurance and references in a single document rather than across attachments that arrive unevenly.

A proposal that arrives without the insurance certificate, without the training record and without the SLA sample is a proposal that arrived incomplete, and the RFP should require completeness before comparison begins.

## FAQ

**What is a commercial cleaning RFP?**
A commercial cleaning RFP is the request for proposal or request for quotation in which a facility manager publishes the cleaning scope, the service expectations, the reporting requirements and the commercial terms before comparing cleaning vendors.

**How many clauses should a commercial cleaning RFP include?**
A serious commercial cleaning RFP centers on twelve mandatory clauses: measurable scope, SLA, penalties, insurance and bonding, personnel and training, products and equipment, pricing and escalation, plus the supporting clauses for evidence, audit, renewal and transition.

**What should a commercial cleaning RFP require for insurance?**
It should require commercial general liability with stated limits, workers compensation, surety bond and indemnification, all with numbers attached rather than general language.

**What is a good SLA for commercial cleaning services?**
A good commercial cleaning SLA pairs a condition score target, a check compliance target, a complaint response window and a deep clean adherence target with defined reporting cadence and contractual consequences.

**How should cleaning penalties be handled in a commercial cleaning RFP?**
Penalties should be capped, scheduled and tied to specific missed targets, with cure periods and separate treatment for recoverable service failures versus compensable performance failures.

**What is a commercial cleaning scope of work?**
A commercial cleaning scope of work is the written description of zones, tasks, frequencies, standards and reporting expectations that a vendor is asked to deliver, and that the buyer is asked to audit.

**How do I write a commercial cleaning RFP for a small office?**
Start with the zone list, write the condition standard and check interval for each zone, define the SLA metrics and penalties, and close with pricing and transition terms; the same clause logic scales down without losing the measurable scope.

**What is the most important clause in a commercial cleaning RFP?**
The measurable scope clause is the root, because every SLA, penalty, insurance and price in the document flows from the way the cleaning work is defined.

**How often should a commercial cleaning RFP be reviewed?**
A cleaning RFP should be reviewed whenever the footprint changes, whenever the scope changes, and on a fixed renewal cycle, with the evidence and reporting cadence reviewed on a weekly or monthly rhythm.

**What is the difference between a commercial cleaning RFP and a cleaning contract?**
An RFP is the buying instrument published before proposals are compared, and it carries the mandatory clauses. A cleaning contract is the award instrument, and it should absorb the winning proposal under the same mandatory clauses.
